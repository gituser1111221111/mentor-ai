# DAVE RAMSEY DEEP RESEARCH

**A Research Foundation for an AI Financial Mentor Based on Dave Ramsey's Publicly Documented Teachings**

---

## Document Purpose and Methodology

This document compiles verified research on Dave Ramsey's financial philosophy, recommendations, decision-making principles, and public statements. It is designed to serve as a standalone knowledge base for an AI financial mentor that emulates Ramsey's publicly documented teachings. It contains 70 numbered research findings organized by financial topic, a synthesis of Ramsey's decision-making principles, a section on disputed claims with the evidence behind competing approaches, a section on research gaps, and a full bibliography with clickable URLs.

**Methodology and source rules used:**

- Ramsey Solutions pages were read from Internet Archive snapshots of the live ramseysolutions.com articles (the live site blocks automated access but works in a normal browser). Every cited Ramsey Solutions URL in the bibliography was verified to exist and to contain the cited content at the time of research (November 2026).
- Statements are flagged as either **Dave Ramsey directly** (from verified "Dave Says" syndicated newspaper columns, documented on-air statements quoted in reputable press, or pages presenting Dave Ramsey's own philosophy) or **Ramsey Solutions editorial** (content published by the company's writing staff, which reflects the company's teachings but is not verifiably Dave Ramsey's own words).
- Where an article was authored by a Ramsey Solutions personality other than Dave Ramsey (e.g., Rachel Cruze, Anthony ONeal), that author is named and the finding is NOT attributed to Dave Ramsey personally.
- Explanations are written in the researcher's own words. Short quotations are used only where the exact wording matters, and are attributed.
- Disputed recommendations are flagged and cross-referenced to the Disputed Claims section.
- Publication dates are the dates shown on the article or its archive snapshot. Ramsey Solutions frequently updates articles without notice, so dates indicate the version read.

**Attribution legend used in each finding:**

| Flag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Dave Ramsey directly | Verifiably Dave Ramsey's own words (Dave Says column, documented on-air quote, or page explicitly presenting "Dave's" philosophy) |
| Ramsey Solutions | Company-published guidance reflecting the Ramsey teaching, author not specified as Dave |
| Ramsey personality | Published by a named Ramsey Solutions personality other than Dave Ramsey |

## SECTION A: The Seven Baby Steps

The Baby Steps are the backbone of Ramsey's entire system: a fixed, sequential, seven-step plan for money. Ramsey Solutions describes them as "the proven plan to paying off debt, saving money, and building wealth" (source: [How to Win With Money in 7 Baby Steps](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-win-with-money-in-7-easy-baby-steps), Rachel Cruze, Ramsey Solutions, updated Apr 5, 2024).

### Finding 1 — The Baby Steps are a fixed seven-step sequence

- **Principle:** Follow seven steps in strict order: (1) save $1,000 starter emergency fund; (2) pay off all debt except the house using the debt snowball; (3) save 3–6 months of expenses in a fully funded emergency fund; (4) invest 15% of household income in retirement; (5) save for children's college; (6) pay off the home mortgage early; (7) build wealth and give.
- **Explanation:** The current canonical presentation of the plan lives on dedicated step pages (2025–2026 redesign) at ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-1 through /step-7. Each step page restates the step, the rationale, and the mechanics. The seed URL provided in the research brief (dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps-b25070) now redirects to this hub.
- **Reasoning:** Ramsey's system is deliberately sequential rather than simultaneous: each step creates the foundation for the next. The starter emergency fund prevents new borrowing while debts are attacked; eliminating debt frees income; the full emergency fund prevents relapse; only then does investing begin.
- **Applies to:** Everyone who adopts the Ramsey plan; Ramsey Solutions markets it as universal.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** The plan assumes the household can eventually reach Step 4 with income to invest. Critics note it is a one-size-fits-all sequence (see Disputed Claims).
- **Source:** [Ramsey Baby Steps hub and step pages](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-1) (Ramsey Solutions, snapshots 2026); [How to Win With Money in 7 Baby Steps](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-win-with-money-in-7-easy-baby-steps) (Rachel Cruze, Apr 5, 2024).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions (the framework itself originates with Dave Ramsey's books and Financial Peace University).
- **Disputed:** No (the sequencing itself is disputed only in its strictness; see Findings 31 and 57 and the Disputed Claims section).

### Finding 2 — Baby Step 1: a $1,000 starter emergency fund comes before everything

- **Principle:** Before paying off any debt, save $1,000 as a "starter emergency fund."
- **Explanation:** The 2026 step page frames the $1,000 as a habit-breaking device: "This step isn't about being ready for any possible emergency—it's about breaking the old habit of relying on debt in an emergency." The page emphasizes speed: save it quickly, then move on.
- **Reasoning:** A small cash buffer stops the debt cycle from worsening when small surprises (flat tire, dental bill) happen during debt payoff. Ramsey's official stance is that $1,000 is intentionally modest during Baby Step 2 because it should create urgency to finish the debt payoff fast (see Finding 44).
- **Applies to:** Anyone beginning the plan, including people with large debt loads.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Ramsey Solutions has acknowledged inflation-era arguments that $1,000 is small; the company's published position remains $1,000 (see Finding 44 for the reasoning). People with extremely unstable income may effectively need more cash on hand (see Finding 16).
- **Source:** [Baby Step 1 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-1) (Ramsey Solutions, snapshot Feb 2026), sections "What Is Baby Step 1?" and "Why It Works."
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** Partially — the adequacy of $1,000 is debated (see Finding 44).

### Finding 3 — Baby Step 2: pay off all non-mortgage debt with the debt snowball, typically in 18–24 months

- **Principle:** Pay off every debt except the mortgage, smallest balance to largest, ignoring interest rates.
- **Explanation:** The current Baby Step 2 page states that on average "families become debt-free in 18–24 months when they use their EveryDollar budget to follow the debt snowball method" (a Ramsey Solutions marketing claim tied to its budgeting app). The snowball mechanics are covered in Section C.
- **Reasoning:** Debt elimination frees the household's income — "your most powerful wealth-building tool" — for saving and investing.
- **Applies to:** All non-mortgage debt: credit cards, car loans, student loans, medical bills, personal loans, payday loans, home equity loans.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** The mortgage is excluded until Baby Step 6. See Findings 10, 30, and 32 for pause and sell-vs-payoff rules.
- **Source:** [Baby Step 2 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-2) (Ramsey Solutions, snapshot Jan 2026), sections "What Is Baby Step 2?" and "How to Pay Off Debt Using the Debt Snowball."
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** The snowball method itself is disputed on mathematical grounds (Finding 29).

### Finding 4 — Baby Step 3: 3–6 months of EXPENSES (not income), calibrated to household stability

- **Principle:** After debt freedom, save 3–6 months of essential expenses.
- **Explanation:** The amount is based on essential spending (housing, utilities, groceries, insurance, transportation, vital bills), not gross income. Calibration guidance from Ramsey Solutions: steady job and two incomes → 3 months; one-income household, self-employed, straight commission, or a chronic medical condition in the household → 6 months.
- **Reasoning:** The fund's job is to keep a job loss or major emergency from sending the family back into debt. More volatile households need a bigger cushion.
- **Applies to:** Everyone after Baby Step 2; kept in a liquid, accessible account (see Finding 42).
- **Exceptions/limitations:** The 2026 Baby Step 3 page adds that if your paycheck varies, aim for 6 months.
- **Source:** [Baby Step 3 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-3) (snapshot Feb 2026); [A Quick Guide to Your Emergency Fund](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/saving/quick-guide-to-your-emergency-fund) (Rachel Cruze, Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021), section "How big should my emergency fund be?"
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions / Rachel Cruze (emergency fund guide).
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 5 — Baby Step 4: invest 15% of GROSS household income in tax-advantaged retirement accounts, in a specific account order

- **Principle:** Invest 15% of gross (pre-tax) household income for retirement, only after Steps 1–3 are complete.
- **Explanation:** The calculation: annual gross household income × 15%, divided by 12 for the monthly target. The published account order: (1) invest up to the employer match in the workplace plan (401(k), 403(b), or TSP); (2) fully fund a Roth IRA for each spouse; (3) if still short of 15%, increase workplace-plan contributions. If the employer offers a Roth 401(k) with good fund options, the entire 15% may go there.
- **Reasoning:** Tax-advantaged accounts maximize compounding; the match is "free money"; Roth IRAs provide tax-free growth.
- **Applies to:** Households that are debt-free except the mortgage with a full emergency fund.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** The 15% rule excludes college savings and extra mortgage payments, which happen in parallel in Steps 5–6. Note that the match is NOT taken during Baby Step 2 (Finding 31).
- **Source:** [Dave's Investing Philosophy](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/daves-investing-philosophy) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021), sections "A Simple Investing Plan"; [Baby Step 4 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-4) (snapshot Feb 2026).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions (page explicitly presents Dave's philosophy).
- **Disputed:** The delay of investing until Step 4 is disputed (Finding 31; Disputed Claims).

### Finding 6 — Baby Step 5: save for kids' college only AFTER retirement investing is at 15%

- **Principle:** After reaching the 15% retirement investing level, save for children's college — retirement always comes first.
- **Explanation:** Recommended vehicles: Educational Savings Accounts (ESAs/Coverdell, $2,000/year contribution cap) and 529 plans (state-run, any state's plan can be used, savings type preferred). Ramsey Solutions explicitly recommends AGAINST prepaid tuition 529 plans because you give up control and potential investment growth. The 2026 step page's stated rationale: "your kids may not even go to college, but you're definitely going to want to retire, and no one else is going to pay for that."
- **Reasoning:** Children have options for college (scholarships, grants, work, affordable schools) but parents cannot borrow for retirement.
- **Applies to:** Parents in Baby Step 5; children of any age (higher contributions needed if starting late).
- **Exceptions/limitations:** The step page emphasizes comparing community college, in-state vs. out-of-state, and public vs. private costs, and calls choosing an unaffordable school "the number one mistake students make."
- **Source:** [Baby Step 5 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-5) (snapshot Jan 2026); [How to Start a College Fund](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/saving/how-to-start-a-college-fund) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Apr 12, 2021); [Dave's Investing Philosophy](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/daves-investing-philosophy).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No (the broader student-loan stance is disputed — Finding 36).

### Finding 7 — Baby Step 6: pay off the mortgage early, without pausing Steps 4 and 5

- **Principle:** Once investing 15% and saving for college, throw all extra budget money at the mortgage principal.
- **Explanation:** Baby Steps 4, 5, and 6 run concurrently. The step page explicitly rejects the mortgage-interest tax deduction as a reason to keep a mortgage: "Paying $10,000 in interest just to save $2,500 on taxes is a bad bargain."
- **Reasoning:** Eliminating the largest payment completes total debt freedom and frees income for extreme investing and giving in Step 7.
- **Applies to:** Homeowners past Step 4.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Do not suspend retirement or college savings to accelerate the mortgage. One or two extra full payments per year is presented as a meaningful accelerator.
- **Source:** [Baby Step 6 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-6) (snapshot Feb 2026), sections "What Is Baby Step 6?" and "Don't Believe the Tax Myth."
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** Partially — paying off low-rate mortgages early vs. investing the difference is a classic debate (see Disputed Claims).

### Finding 8 — Baby Step 7: build wealth and give — with a $10 million simplification threshold

- **Principle:** After the mortgage is gone, money is for three things: giving, investing, and having fun.
- **Explanation:** The 2026 step page says to "keep your plan simple. Stick with mutual funds and debt-free real estate. Once you've built $10 million in net worth, you and your investing pro can talk about other options." It also instructs continuing to max out Roth IRAs and 401(k)s, buying investment real estate only with cash (with reserves for taxes, insurance, repairs), and giving generously.
- **Reasoning:** With no payments, wealth compounds; the end goal of wealth in Ramsey's system is generosity and legacy ("live and give like no one else"), not accumulation for its own sake.
- **Applies to:** Households that complete Step 6.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** The $10 million threshold for considering more complex investments is a Ramsey Solutions editorial guideline, not a rule Dave Ramsey has been verified stating on air.
- **Source:** [Baby Step 7 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-7) (snapshot Feb 2026), section "How to Do Baby Step 7."
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 9 — Sequential focus: one money goal at a time, because behavior beats math

- **Principle:** Do not work multiple Baby Steps simultaneously (except Steps 4–6 by design); concentrated focus is the engine of the plan.
- **Explanation:** In a verified Dave Says column, Ramsey told a caller with $51,000 in student loans and a $140,000 income who wanted to fund IRAs while finishing Baby Step 2: "If you're still on Baby Step 2, you need to stop all saving and investing and attack that last piece of debt with a vengeance... There's power in behavior modification on a short-term basis that supersedes the power of mathematics."
- **Reasoning:** Spreading effort across goals dilutes intensity and lets people quit; quick, visible progress on a single goal changes behavior, which Ramsey considers the real problem.
- **Applies to:** Especially Baby Steps 1–3.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Steps 4, 5, and 6 are explicitly simultaneous; insurance must be maintained throughout (Finding 67).
- **Source:** [Dave Says: Investing While Paying Down Debt?](https://cbn.com/article/finances/dave-says-investing-while-paying-down-debt) (Dave Ramsey, CBN, syndicated column). Independent analysis supporting the checklist/focus approach: [Kitces — Why Checklist-Style Financial Planning Works](https://www.kitces.com/blog/checklist-style-financial-planning-dave-ramsey-baby-steps-money-guy-show-order-of-operations/) (Derek Tharp & Michael Kitces, Sep 21, 2022).
- **Attribution:** Dave Ramsey directly (column).
- **Disputed:** The strict sequencing (e.g., delaying investing and even employer match) is disputed — see Finding 31.

### Finding 10 — Legitimate reasons to pause the debt snowball: babies, job loss, health crises

- **Principle:** The debt snowball can be temporarily paused for three life events: a coming baby, a job loss, or a family health crisis.
- **Explanation:** Published guidance: for a baby, make minimum payments and stockpile cash until mother and child are home healthy; for job loss, enter "survival mode" (Four Walls first, minimum payments if possible, side gigs to avoid draining the emergency fund); for a health crisis, prioritize care and cash-flow medical bills.
- **Reasoning:** A cash pile protects against the specific, predictable risk window; the snowball resumes when stability returns.
- **Applies to:** Anyone in Baby Step 2 hit by these events.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Do not pause for burnout, wants (e.g., a new fence), or predictable annual expenses — those belong in the budget or sinking funds (Findings 18–21). Pausing for "an unexpected or expensive situation" generally is allowed if needed, then resume.
- **Source:** [When to Pause Your Debt Snowball](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/3-reasons-to-pause-debt-snowball) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Mar 10, 2021), sections "When should you pause the debt snowball?" and the three numbered reasons.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

## SECTION B: Budgeting (Including Irregular Income)

### Finding 11 — Zero-based budgeting: income minus expenses equals zero

- **Principle:** Every dollar of income is assigned a job (spending, saving, giving, investing) before the month begins, so income minus expenses equals zero.
- **Explanation:** Zero does NOT mean an empty bank account — Ramsey Solutions advises keeping a small buffer of roughly $100–300 in checking. The zero means every dollar is "named" and purposed. In a Dave Ramsey-branded article, the company states: "Dave recommends telling every dollar where it should go—before the month begins—using a zero-based budget."
- **Reasoning:** Unassigned money leaks; a plan made in advance puts the person in control and reveals money people didn't know they had.
- **Applies to:** Every household, every month, regardless of income level or regularity.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** A new budget must be made each month because needs change; seasonal/irregular expenses require forward planning (Finding 20).
- **Source:** [How to Budget Using Simple, Zero-Based Budgeting](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-budget) (Ramsey Solutions, snapshot Apr 2021); [How to Make a Zero-Based Budget](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-make-a-zero-based-budget) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions (with explicit Dave Ramsey framing in the how-to-budget page).
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 12 — Budget before the month begins; write a new budget every single month

- **Principle:** The budget is written before the month starts, and re-made monthly.
- **Explanation:** Both the zero-based budget article and the irregular-income guide instruct listing that month's specific bills (including quarterly or annual items due that month) and planning spending in advance. "Your needs will change from month to month, which is why you need to stick to it and make a new budget every single month."
- **Reasoning:** Monthly re-planning keeps the plan matched to reality and maintains the habit.
- **Applies to:** All budgeters.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None stated; copy-forward tools (EveryDollar) are recommended for speed.
- **Source:** [How to Make a Zero-Based Budget](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-make-a-zero-based-budget) (Ramsey Solutions), sections 1–3.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 13 — The Four Walls: food, utilities, shelter, transportation — in that order

- **Principle:** When money is tight, cover the "Four Walls" first: food, utilities, shelter, and transportation.
- **Explanation:** The Four Walls are the survival priorities used when building a budget, during a job-loss "survival mode," and when deciding what to pay when income is short. Everything else (including debt payments beyond minimums, and nonessentials) comes after.
- **Reasoning:** These four keep the family alive and able to work; protecting them prevents catastrophe while other goals are paused.
- **Applies to:** Budget construction generally; critically during income interruptions.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Insurance and minimum debt payments are listed right after the Four Walls in the expense-priority guidance (Finding 14).
- **Source:** [How to Make a Zero-Based Budget](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-make-a-zero-based-budget), section 2; [How to Budget With an Irregular Income](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-budget-an-irregular-income) (Ramsey Solutions, Oct 13, 2023), section 2; [When to Pause Your Debt Snowball](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/3-reasons-to-pause-debt-snowball).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 14 — The budget line priority order: giving, saving, Four Walls, essentials, nonessentials

- **Principle:** Expenses are listed in a fixed priority order: giving first, then saving, then the Four Walls, then other essential expenses, then nonessentials.
- **Explanation:** The irregular-income guide specifies: giving 10% (church or charity), then saving (or debt attack, depending on Baby Step), then the Four Walls, then essentials like insurance, debt payments and childcare, then nonessentials like streaming and restaurants, plus a miscellaneous line.
- **Reasoning:** Giving and saving are put first because "if you don't make saving and giving a priority at the start of your budget, chances are you won't make them a priority period."
- **Applies to:** Monthly budget construction, especially with constrained or irregular income.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** During a job-loss survival mode, giving and nonessentials effectively drop out and the Four Walls lead.
- **Source:** [How to Budget With an Irregular Income](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-budget-an-irregular-income) (Ramsey Solutions, Oct 13, 2023), section 2; [How to Make a Zero-Based Budget](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-make-a-zero-based-budget), section 2.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 15 — Irregular income: budget on your LOWEST monthly income

- **Principle:** With variable income, set the budget using the lowest realistic monthly income from recent history, not the average.
- **Explanation:** "Plan low... if you budget low, you can always go up from there. But guessing high and having to back off later—that spells trouble." Look back at recent pay stubs and use the lowest month; if new to variable income, estimate the lowest month conservatively.
- **Reasoning:** Under-budgeting creates surplus (manageable); over-budgeting creates mid-month cuts and failure (demotivating).
- **Applies to:** Hourly workers with variable schedules, commission salespeople, gig workers, freelancers, tipped employees.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** For commission earners specifically, Ramsey Solutions now recommends a "realistic income floor" (worst realistic month over the past 6–12 months) rather than the absolute worst month — budgeting to zero-commission months is "probably a little too conservative" (Finding 16).
- **Source:** [How to Budget With an Irregular Income](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-budget-an-irregular-income) (Ramsey Solutions, Oct 13, 2023), section 1; [How to Budget With a Low Income](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-budget-money-with-low-income) (Rachel Cruze, Aug 29, 2022), step 1.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 16 — Commission income: income floor + prioritized list + peak-and-valley fund

- **Principle:** For commission earners: (a) budget on a realistic income floor; (b) keep a pre-made prioritized spending list for every dollar above the floor; (c) smooth swings with a "peak-and-valley fund."
- **Explanation:** The April 2026 Ramsey Solutions commission guide: find the floor by reviewing 6–12 months of checks and using the worst realistic month (or average of bad months). Above-floor dollars work down the prioritized list in order — the list is written BEFORE money arrives and might read: (1) finish Baby Step 1, (2) debt snowball payments, (3) Baby Step 3 savings, and so on, depending on current Baby Step. Instead of a "buffer fund" that pays a fixed salary, Ramsey recommends a peak-and-valley fund: deposit in great months, withdraw in short months (example: $5,000 budgeted expenses, $4,000 commission, pull $1,000 from the fund).
- **Reasoning:** "Every extra dollar already has a job waiting for it," which prevents lifestyle creep in big months and panic in slow months.
- **Applies to:** Commission salespeople, real estate agents, insurance agents; adaptable to freelancers and gig workers with lumpy income.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Self-employed/commission earners should also set aside 25–30% of each check for taxes (quarterly estimated payments) in a separate account "and treat it like it's gone."
- **Source:** [How to Budget on Commission Income](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-budget-on-commission-income) (Ramsey Solutions, Apr 16, 2026), sections "Should You Budget Based on Your Lowest Commission Month?", "How Do You Handle Income Above Your Budget Baseline?", "How Much Should You Keep in a Commission Buffer Fund?", "How Do You Handle Taxes on Commission Income?"
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 17 — Mixed stable + variable income: budget on the predictable income (Dave Says, directly)

- **Principle:** When one spouse has regular income and the other has variable income, build the budget on the predictable paycheck and treat the variable income as supplemental.
- **Explanation:** In a verified Dave Says column, a military spouse (stable income) with a commission-based wife asked how to budget. Dave replied: "Start with the income that you know is predictable... begin setting up your written monthly budget based on what your husband makes. Then, you need to determine YOUR absolute minimum income for a given month. Once this is in place, you can prioritize the remaining expenses, savings and investing based on your income."
- **Reasoning:** Anchor the plan to the guaranteed floor; layered priorities handle the variable portion.
- **Applies to:** Households with one predictable and one variable income stream.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None stated in the column.
- **Source:** [Dave Says: How Do You Make a Budget With an Irregular Income?](https://cbn.com/article/finances/dave-says-how-do-you-make-budget-irregular-income) (Dave Ramsey, CBN, Mar 24, 2023).
- **Attribution:** Dave Ramsey directly.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 18 — Predictable slow seasons are NOT emergencies — use a budget line, not the emergency fund (Dave Says, directly)

- **Principle:** Seasonal income dips that you can foresee (a painter's three slow months, Christmas, birthdays) are planned expenses, not emergencies.
- **Explanation:** In a verified Dave Says column, a couple whose commercial-painting business has three lean months wanted to skip Baby Step 2 and jump to a 6-month emergency fund. Dave refused: "if it's something predictable, something that happens at the same time every year, it is not an emergency... It would be a line in the budget where you set money aside because you know something's coming." He added that the husband should also rework his business model so income doesn't dry up completely in the down months.
- **Reasoning:** The emergency fund is for the unknown; known timing requires planning (sinking-fund logic). Skipping Baby Steps to double-cover a predictable event breaks the plan's sequencing.
- **Applies to:** Seasonal workers, business owners with cyclical revenue, anyone with known annual expenses.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Setting aside household money for the down time is fine; the stronger fix is earning income during the lull.
- **Source:** [Dave Says column, Madill Record](https://www.madillrecord.net/node/10303) (Dave Ramsey, syndicated "DAVE SAYS" column, published Aug 10, 2023), first letter.
- **Attribution:** Dave Ramsey directly.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 19 — Sinking funds: save monthly for KNOWN future expenses; the emergency fund is for the UNKNOWN

- **Principle:** A sinking fund is money set aside monthly for a specific, planned future expense; it is deliberately separate from the emergency fund.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey personality Rachel Cruze's article: "The sinking fund is for the known. The emergency fund is for the unknown." Example uses: tires, Christmas, vet bills, weddings, plane tickets, school supplies, vacations, home remodels. Multiple specific funds beat one blended savings account because "sooner or later, the lines will start to blur."
- **Reasoning:** Known expenses that arrive as budget-busting lumps are failures of planning, not emergencies; small monthly amounts make big expenses painless.
- **Applies to:** All budgeters; the mechanism behind Finding 18.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None stated.
- **Source:** [What Is a Sinking Fund and How Do You Create One?](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/saving/stop-the-panic-sinking-fund) (Rachel Cruze, Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021), sections "Sinking Fund vs. Emergency Fund" and "What Is a Sinking Fund?"
- **Attribution:** Ramsey personality (Rachel Cruze) — reflects the company's teaching, not verifiably Dave's own words.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 20 — Annual/irregular bills: mine last year's statements, spread them into monthly amounts, and keep a buffer line

- **Principle:** Find every non-monthly expense from the past year (insurance premiums, HOA dues, tag renewals, dental cleanings), then either pre-load them into the months they're due or divide them into monthly savings amounts.
- **Explanation:** The Ramsey Solutions guide gives a worked example: $200 quarterly HOA dues becomes $67/month starting months in advance. Add a "surprise expenses" buffer category (even $50/month); unused buffer money goes to the current Baby Step.
- **Reasoning:** Irregular bills "sneak under the radar" and bust budgets; a look back plus a small buffer converts surprises into plan items.
- **Applies to:** All budgeters; essential for irregular-income households.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None stated.
- **Source:** [How to Budget for Big Expenses That Only Happen Once or Twice a Year](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/saving/how-to-budget-for-big-expenses) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Mar 10, 2021), sections "Revisit Your Past," "Flash Forward to the Future," "Build in a Buffer."
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 21 — The six-step irregular-income budget cycle (track, adjust, re-budget)

- **Principle:** The irregular-income budget follows six steps: (1) list income at the lowest estimate; (2) list expenses in priority order; (3) subtract to zero (zero-based); (4) track expenses all month; (5) adjust every paycheck; (6) make a new budget before the next month begins.
- **Explanation:** Tracking and per-paycheck adjustment are what make a low-planned budget work: when income beats the floor, the surplus is assigned (to the prioritized list or budget lines); when it falls short, nonessentials are cut.
- **Reasoning:** A static plan fails variable income; a monitored, adjusted plan stays at zero all month.
- **Applies to:** All variable-income households.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None stated.
- **Source:** [How to Budget With an Irregular Income](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-budget-an-irregular-income) (Ramsey Solutions, Oct 13, 2023) — full six-step structure.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 22 — Low income is not an excuse to skip the budget

- **Principle:** Households with low income use the same budgeting method as everyone else; the budget matters more, not less.
- **Explanation:** Rachel Cruze's low-income guide repeats the standard steps (income, expenses, Four Walls first, miscellaneous line) and adds tactics: side hustles, negotiating bills, meal planning, cutting subscriptions, and increasing income. For irregular income, "just use your lowest monthly income as a place to start."
- **Reasoning:** Every dollar's job matters more when there are fewer dollars; a plan plus more income is the way out.
- **Applies to:** Low-wage, fixed-income, and student households.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None stated.
- **Source:** [How to Budget With a Low Income](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-budget-money-with-low-income) (Rachel Cruze, Aug 29, 2022).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey personality (Rachel Cruze).
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 23 — Handling a monthly income shortfall: cut nonessentials, protect the Four Walls

- **Principle:** When income comes in below the budgeted floor, go back into the budget, adjust income, and cut nonessential lines until the budget is back to zero.
- **Explanation:** The commission guide's example: budgeted $5,000, commission $4,000 → pull $1,000 from the peak-and-valley fund or cut nonessentials (restaurants, streaming, fun money) — the Four Walls and essentials stay funded. "In a tight month, they might not make the cut."
- **Reasoning:** The plan flexes on wants, never on needs; this is the practical application of the priority order in Finding 14.
- **Applies to:** Any month income underperforms.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** If shortfalls are chronic, the issue is the income floor estimate or income itself, not the budget mechanics.
- **Source:** [How to Budget on Commission Income](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-budget-on-commission-income) (Ramsey Solutions, Apr 16, 2026), sections on below-floor months and high-income months.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 24 — The envelope system for leaky spending categories

- **Principle:** For categories where spending is hard to control (groceries, restaurants, clothing, gas), use cash in physical envelopes.
- **Explanation:** The how-to-budget page offers the envelope system as the tool "for those wanting to curb spending in some key budget areas," alongside EveryDollar. When an envelope is empty, spending in that category stops for the month.
- **Reasoning:** Cash is felt; swipes are not. This is the behavioral underpinning of Ramsey's cash preference (Finding 37).
- **Applies to:** Habitual overspenders and leaky categories.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Debit cards are approved for online purchases (hotels, flights) where cash is impractical.
- **Source:** [How to Budget Using Simple, Zero-Based Budgeting](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-budget) (Ramsey Solutions, snapshot Apr 2021), "Envelope Systems" section.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 25 — A miscellaneous line absorbs surprises; leftovers go to the current Baby Step

- **Principle:** Keep a "miscellaneous" budget line for small unexpected costs; whatever remains at month's end is thrown at the current Baby Step.
- **Explanation:** The zero-based budget article: the misc line is the "catch all" for a kid's party invite or forgotten water filter — not a shoe fund. The big-expenses guide adds the surprise-expenses buffer variant ($50/month example) with leftovers funding the current goal.
- **Reasoning:** Small surprises shouldn't bust the plan or touch the emergency fund; leftovers stay purposeful.
- **Applies to:** Every budget.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None stated.
- **Source:** [How to Make a Zero-Based Budget](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-make-a-zero-based-budget); [How to Budget for Big Expenses](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/saving/how-to-budget-for-big-expenses).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 26 — Fun money belongs in the budget

- **Principle:** Include guilt-free discretionary/fun spending as a planned category rather than pretending it away.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions budgeting content consistently includes personal spending and entertainment lines after essentials, and a dedicated article argues "Why You Need Some Fun Money in Your Budget" (referenced on the how-to-budget page). The irregular-income guide notes extras "may not be able" to be funded every month in low months.
- **Reasoning:** A budget with no fun is abandoned; planned fun keeps the plan sustainable.
- **Applies to:** All budgeters.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Fun money is first on the cut list in short months and during gazelle-intense Baby Step 2.
- **Source:** [How to Budget](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-budget) ("From the Blog" references); [How to Budget With an Irregular Income](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-budget-an-irregular-income).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

## SECTION C: Debt Repayment

### Finding 27 — The debt snowball: smallest balance first, regardless of interest rate

- **Principle:** List all non-mortgage debts smallest to largest; pay minimums on everything except the smallest; attack the smallest with everything extra; roll each freed payment into the next-smallest debt.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions' worked example: four debts ($500 medical, $2,500 card, $7,000 car, $10,000 student loan) plus $500/month extra from a side job and cut expenses ("You are gazelle intense"). The $500 medical bill dies in month one; the freed $550 rolls to the card; the card dies in about four months; $748/month then kills the car loan in 10 months; $844/month retires the student loan in about 12 months — $20,000 of debt gone in 27 months.
- **Reasoning:** Momentum and visible progress; each closed account is a motivational win that keeps the person attacking.
- **Applies to:** All non-mortgage debt, at any income level.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Do not begin until Baby Step 1 is done and you are current on all bills. Mortgages wait for Baby Step 6.
- **Source:** [How the Debt Snowball Method Works](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/how-the-debt-snowball-method-works) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 26, 2021), sections "How Does the Debt Snowball Method Work?" and "An Example of the Debt Snowball"; [How to Get Out of Debt With the Debt Snowball Plan](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/get-out-of-debt-with-the-debt-snowball-plan) (Ramsey Solutions, updated May 31, 2024).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** Yes, on mathematical grounds — see Finding 29.

### Finding 28 — Why Ramsey ignores interest rates: "personal finance is 80% behavior and only 20% head knowledge"

- **Principle:** The snowball deliberately ignores interest rates because behavior, not math, determines whether people actually get out of debt.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions' published reasoning: "Sure, you might think paying off the debt with the highest interest rate first would save you more money in the end... But here's the deal: Personal finance is 80% behavior and only 20% head knowledge." Starting with the biggest debt means no visible progress for a long time, so people "lose steam and give up"; starting small produces quick wins that "light a fire" under the remaining debts.
- **Reasoning:** People in debt got there through behavior; only changed behavior gets them out, and changed behavior requires motivation, which requires wins.
- **Applies to:** The framing behind every Ramsey debt recommendation.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** The company concedes the avalanche (highest-rate-first) is mathematically cheaper — the dispute is over completion rates, not arithmetic.
- **Source:** [How to Get Out of Debt With the Debt Snowball Plan](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/get-out-of-debt-with-the-debt-snowball-plan) (updated May 31, 2024), section "Why Ignore the Interest Rates?"; [Your Top Debt Snowball Questions Answered](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/top-5-debt-snowball-questions-answered) (updated Mar 5, 2021), Q1.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions (the 80/20 formulation originates with Dave Ramsey).
- **Disputed:** The behavioral claim has research support (Finding 29) but the total-cost tradeoff is real — see Disputed Claims.

### Finding 29 — Independent evidence on snowball vs. avalanche

- **Principle:** The snowball's behavioral advantage is documented in academic research, while the avalanche is mathematically cheaper.
- **Explanation:** Kellogg School (Northwestern) researchers David Gal and Blakeley McShane analyzed a debt-settlement dataset of ~6,000 consumers and found that those who closed small accounts first were more likely to eliminate their debt entirely: "closing debt accounts — independent of the dollar balances of the closed accounts — predicted successful debt elimination." Kitces' analysis frames it as: the snowball is "not defensible on mathematical grounds but is defensible from a behavioral perspective," because paying off even one small debt "changes one's self-image to someone who can pay off their debt."
- **Reasoning:** Small wins change self-perception and sustain effort.
- **Applies to:** Weighing snowball vs. avalanche for a real household.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Neither source claims the snowball minimizes interest paid; the avalanche does. The tradeoff is total interest vs. probability of completion.
- **Source:** [The 'snowball approach' to debt — Kellogg School of Management](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/news/blog/2012/08/07/the-snowball-approach-to-debt/) (Ray Boyer, Aug 7, 2012, on Gal & McShane, Journal of Marketing Research); [Kitces — Dave Ramsey's Behavioral Advice Ingenuity](https://www.kitces.com/blog/dave-ramsey-show-financial-peace-university-behavioral-finance-criticism-debt-snowball-bengen-12-percent-return-withdrawl/) (Derek Tharp & Michael Kitces, Jan 27, 2021), section "Ramsey's Debt Snowball Method."
- **Attribution:** Independent research (not Ramsey).
- **Disputed:** N/A — this IS the dispute documentation.

### Finding 30 — What goes in the snowball: ALL non-mortgage debt, because "debt is anything you owe to anyone"

- **Principle:** Include every non-mortgage obligation: student loans, medical bills, car loans, credit cards, home equity loans, personal loans, payday loans.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey's definition is deliberately broad — "debt is anything you owe to anyone else" — so informal debts (family loans, payment plans) count. The mortgage is the only exclusion (deferred to Baby Step 6).
- **Reasoning:** Any owed money is a claim on your income.
- **Applies to:** Snowball list construction.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None; the home equity loan inclusion is notable because many conventional advisors treat secured debt differently.
- **Source:** [How to Get Out of Debt With the Debt Snowball Plan](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/get-out-of-debt-with-the-debt-snowball-plan), section "What Debts Should I Include in My Debt Snowball?"; [Debt Snowball vs. Debt Avalanche](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/debt-snowball-vs-debt-avalanche) (Ramsey Solutions, updated May 24, 2023).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 31 — Stop ALL retirement investing during Baby Step 2 — including the employer match

- **Principle:** While paying off non-mortgage debt, suspend every retirement contribution, even where an employer match is forfeited.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions: "if you've got some debt in your back pocket, we recommend holding off on all investing—including your company's 401(k)—until you reach Baby Step 4... as hard as it seems, it's best to wait." The FAQ is blunter: "Even if you get a company match, don't take it while you're eliminating your debts. Remember, this is only temporary." The company even reframes the forfeited match as motivational fuel ("that queasy feeling is what's going to keep you working hard until you're completely debt-free").
- **Reasoning:** Split focus slows debt payoff and prolongs the debt period; intensity and a single goal change behavior. (See Finding 9's Dave Says quote.)
- **Applies to:** Baby Step 2.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None published. This is one of the most-criticized Ramsey positions (see Disputed Claims — most fee-only advisors recommend capturing the match even during debt payoff).
- **Source:** [Should I Take My 401(k) Company Match?](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/401k-company-match) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021), section "Should I Take My Company Match?"; [Your Top Debt Snowball Questions Answered](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/top-5-debt-snowball-questions-answered), Q3.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** YES — heavily. See Disputed Claims.

### Finding 32 — When to sell the financed car: the 24-month and 50%-of-net-worth rules

- **Principle:** Sell a vehicle (or other financed asset) if it cannot be paid off within 24 months, or if its value exceeds roughly half your net worth.
- **Explanation:** Published snowball FAQ: "if you need more than 24 months to pay off your car, it's time to sell"; and "if it's more than 50%" of net worth, "you're investing too much into something that goes down in value." The same logic extends to boats and other non-home assets. Items a few months from payoff can be kept.
- **Reasoning:** Overweighting a depreciating asset while in debt is a wealth killer; the payment relief accelerates the snowball.
- **Applies to:** Cars, boats, recreational assets during Baby Step 2.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Keep items that will be debt-free within a few months.
- **Source:** [Your Top Debt Snowball Questions Answered](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/top-5-debt-snowball-questions-answered), Q2.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 33 — Debt consolidation is rejected: it moves debt around instead of eliminating it

- **Principle:** Avoid debt consolidation loans and balance transfers; they lengthen the debt timeline and do not fix behavior.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions' published points: consolidation is "a refinanced loan with extended repayment terms" (you stay in debt longer); a lower rate "isn't always a guarantee" and promotional balance-transfer rates expire; and "your interest rate isn't the main problem. Your spending habits are the problem." Debt settlement companies are called scam-prone ("the cold sores of the financial world" per the credit-card article) — the FTC receives top consumer complaints about debt-relief outfits.
- **Reasoning:** Restructuring without behavior change leads to re-borrowing; the only durable fix is the snowball plus budgeting.
- **Applies to:** Anyone considering consolidation, settlement, or 401(k) loans to "simplify" debt.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Refinancing student loans to a lower rate is marketed by Ramsey Solutions' own recommended partners, which critics note sits in tension with the anti-consolidation stance (see Disputed Claims).
- **Source:** [The Truth About Debt Consolidation](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/debt-consolidation-truth) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Mar 9, 2021); [How to Pay Off Credit Card Debt](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/how-to-pay-off-credit-card-debt) (updated Feb 25, 2021), section "What Are Other Credit Card Repayment Methods—and Do They Work?"
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** Partially — consolidation CAN reduce interest costs for disciplined borrowers; Ramsey's objection is behavioral. See Disputed Claims.

### Finding 34 — Never cash out retirement to pay debt (with a bankruptcy/foreclosure exception)

- **Principle:** Do not withdraw from IRAs/401(k)s to pay consumer debt; the exception is avoiding bankruptcy or foreclosure.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions: "Dave Ramsey says you shouldn't take money out of your IRA early unless it's to avoid bankruptcy or foreclosure." Mechanics cited: early withdrawals before 59½ trigger a 10% penalty plus income tax (401(k) withdrawals typically lose ~20% to withholding plus penalty). In a verified Dave Says column, Ramsey told a veteran with a $3,200 forgotten TSP to roll it into an IRA rather than withdraw: taking it out would cost "30% to 40%" in penalty and taxes — "that's kind of like asking, 'Would it be a good idea to borrow $3,200 at 30% interest to pay off debt?' Of course not!" 401(k) LOANS are also rejected ("Never borrow from your 401(k)... you're stealing from your own future").
- **Reasoning:** The tax/penalty haircut makes retirement raids the most expensive possible payoff funding; retirement money is protected and compound time is irreplaceable.
- **Applies to:** Anyone tempted to use retirement funds for debt.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Bankruptcy/foreclosure avoidance is the stated exception. Rollovers (not withdrawals) between qualified accounts are fine within 60 days.
- **Source:** [Should I Take Money Out of My IRA to Pay Off Debt?](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/ira-withdrawal-to-pay-off-debt) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021); [Dave Says column, Madill Record](https://www.madillrecord.net/node/10303) (Aug 10, 2023), TSP letter; [How to Pay Off Credit Card Debt](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/how-to-pay-off-credit-card-debt).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions + Dave Ramsey directly (TSP column).
- **Disputed:** No (broadly accepted, though some advisors would note 401(k) loans are cheaper than they're portrayed).

### Finding 35 — Credit card debt: the minimum-payment trap and the "quick fix" warning list

- **Principle:** Credit card debt is eliminated by the snowball, not by minimum payments, consolidation, settlement, or raiding assets.
- **Explanation:** The credit-card payoff article walks through why minimums stall (growing balances, "after a while... you've got $10,000 of debt with nothing to show for it"), then lists the rejected shortcuts: debt consolidation (longer terms), debt settlement (fees, scams), 401(k) loans (penalties, "stealing from your own future"), and home equity loans (putting the house at risk for consumption debt).
- **Reasoning:** There is "no quick fix"; the time-tested solution is income minus outgo applied to smallest-balance-first with intensity.
- **Applies to:** Revolving credit card balances.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None.
- **Source:** [How to Pay Off Credit Card Debt](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/how-to-pay-off-credit-card-debt) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021), sections "Why Credit Card Debt Is Bad" and "What Are Other Credit Card Repayment Methods?"
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 36 — Student loans: there are no "good" student loans; pay for college without them

- **Principle:** Student loan debt is treated as a crisis to be avoided entirely — cash-flow college via scholarships, grants, work, community college, in-state public schools, and living at home.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions' student-loan hub (authored by former Ramsey personality Anthony ONeal in the version captured) frames loans as a $1.6+ trillion trap and urges students not to take them. The 2026 Baby Step 5 page cites National Center for Education Statistics data that 62% of 2020–21 undergraduates took no federal loans, and frames the #1 mistake as "choosing a school they can't afford." For borrowers already stuck, loans go into the snowball like any other debt.
- **Reasoning:** Loans delay life milestones (home, kids, retirement) and fund an overpriced system; debt-free degree paths exist.
- **Applies to:** Students and parents planning college; graduates repaying loans.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Kitces documents that Ramsey's debt-free strategies are widely considered strained for expensive professional schools (medical, law) — Ramsey's on-air response points to MD/PhD programs, fellowships, scholarships, and need-based aid rather than telling callers to skip medical school. See Disputed Claims.
- **Source:** [Overcoming the Student Loan Crisis](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/student-loan-crisis) (Anthony ONeal, Ramsey Solutions, updated Mar 4, 2021); [Baby Step 5 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-5); [Kitces — Ramsey's Advice On Student Loans](https://www.kitces.com/blog/dave-ramsey-show-financial-peace-university-behavioral-finance-criticism-debt-snowball-bengen-12-percent-return-withdrawl/).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey personality (ONeal article) / Ramsey Solutions / Dave Ramsey (on-air position per Kitces).
- **Disputed:** Yes for professional-school contexts. See Disputed Claims.

### Finding 37 — Credit cards: cut them up; use cash and debit; rewards are not worth it

- **Principle:** People in (or escaping) debt should not keep credit cards at all; Ramsey Solutions argues even "responsible" rewards use loses money.
- **Explanation:** The rewards article's argument: card companies induce overspending ("get you to spend way more than what you would ever spend in cold, hard cash, and then they reward you with a teeny-tiny percentage"), miles have blackout dates and seat limits, and the average rewards rate is trivially small. The cash-vs-card article rebutts common excuses point by point and approves debit cards for online purchases. Independent research supports the core spending effect: people spend more with cards than cash (also acknowledged by Ramsey critic Jim Dahle, White Coat Investor: "People spend more when using credit cards" is one of the things Ramsey gets RIGHT).
- **Reasoning:** The behavioral risk of swiping outweighs any rewards math; the game is rigged by the issuer.
- **Applies to:** Everyone following the Ramsey plan; strongest for people with any history of revolving balances.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Debit for online travel/hotels; critics note rewards-card use is mathematically profitable for the minority who pay in full every month (see Disputed Claims).
- **Source:** [Are Credit Card Points Worth It?](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/credit-card-rewards-yeah-right) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021); [Cash vs. Credit Card: Which Should I Use?](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/cash-vs-credit-card) (updated Feb 25, 2021); [White Coat Investor — Dave Ramsey Criticism](https://www.whitecoatinvestor.com/22-things-dave-ramsey-gets-wrong-and-right/) (Jim Dahle, MD, Jul 31, 2017), "Things Dave Gets Right" #10.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions (position); independent support noted.
- **Disputed:** Partially — for disciplined full-balance payers. See Disputed Claims.

### Finding 38 — The credit score: an "I love debt score" you are better off without

- **Principle:** Ramsey teaches that a credit score only measures your relationship with debt, and that you can live without one entirely.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions' published position: the score is calculated from payment history (35%), amounts owed (30%), length of history (15%), new credit (10%), credit mix (10%) — all debt metrics — so "the best way to 'improve your score' is by ditching your credit altogether... Your financial success depends on the numbers in your bank account, not the numbers on your credit report." The "living without a credit score" guide covers mortgages via manual underwriting (large down payment, employment history, rental/utility payment history), renting (first/last month plus deposit, landlord references), and job screening in financial industries.
- **Reasoning:** If you never borrow, you don't need a borrowing reputation; building wealth is about assets, not a lender's opinion.
- **Applies to:** Households committed to never borrowing except a 15-year mortgage (or none).
- **Exceptions/limitations:** The company itself notes the guidance applies to people with NO score; those with a bad score from unpaid debts must clean up the debts. Practical costs of no-score living (manual underwriting friction, apartment applications) are documented by critics. See Disputed Claims.
- **Source:** [The Best Way to Improve Your Credit Score](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/the-number-one-way-credit-score) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021); [Living Without a Credit Score](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/living-without-credit-score) (updated Mar 4, 2021).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions (the "I love debt score" phrase is Dave Ramsey's signature term).
- **Disputed:** YES — see Disputed Claims.

### Finding 39 — The avalanche, defined fairly: highest interest rate first

- **Principle:** The debt avalanche (debt stacking) pays debts highest-rate to lowest-rate; Ramsey Solutions acknowledges it saves interest but rejects it on completion-risk grounds.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions defines the avalanche accurately (e.g., $15,000 at 23% before $10,000 at 5%) and concedes "the debt avalanche method can save you money in interest" — its objections are behavioral: you may face the biggest balance first, "which can keep you from making progress," and it lacks the snowball's early wins.
- **Reasoning:** Stated above (Finding 28).
- **Applies to:** Households choosing a payoff order.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Ramsey's own snowball-vs-avalanche article has been criticized as editorially slanted (it describes the avalanche's steps as "fuzzy" and "doesn't work"); an AI mentor should present the avalanche's real advantage (lower total interest) honestly.
- **Source:** [Debt Snowball vs. Debt Avalanche](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/debt-snowball-vs-debt-avalanche) (Ramsey Solutions, updated May 24, 2023), "Key Takeaways" and "What Is the Debt Avalanche Method?"
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** The framing is disputed; the tradeoff itself is factual.

## SECTION D: Emergency Funds

### Finding 40 — What counts as an emergency: unexpected, necessary, urgent

- **Principle:** Test any surprise expense with three questions: Is it unexpected? Is it necessary? Is it urgent? The more "yes" answers, the more it justifies emergency-fund use.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions' examples of true emergencies: car wreck, hospital visit, leaky roof, job loss, HVAC failure in winter. Explicit non-examples: a TV on clearance, a BOGO shoe sale. Predictable events (Christmas, annual premiums, known car repairs) are budget/sinking-fund items, not emergencies.
- **Reasoning:** The fund is a defined-purpose tool; blurring its purpose (see Finding 41) destroys it.
- **Applies to:** Every emergency-fund decision.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None.
- **Source:** [A Quick Guide to Your Emergency Fund](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/saving/quick-guide-to-your-emergency-fund) (Rachel Cruze, Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021), sections "What is an emergency fund?" and the three-question test.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey personality (Rachel Cruze).
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 41 — Overspending is not an emergency (Dave Says, directly)

- **Principle:** Blowing past a budget category is fixed by cutting another category, never by raiding the emergency fund.
- **Explanation:** In a verified Dave Says column, a husband wanted emergency-fund money for busted home-improvement budgets; Dave sided with the wife: "I hate to break it to you, but overspending is not an emergency... If something happens on a pretty regular basis, it's a predictable event. That means you need to budget a larger amount." His example: $200 budgeted for car repairs, $250 bill → cut eating out to cover it. "We never touched the emergency fund for anything except big, unexpected, scary stuff."
- **Reasoning:** Protects the fund's integrity; forces honest category sizing.
- **Applies to:** All budget overruns.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Genuine unexpected, necessary, urgent events qualify (Finding 40).
- **Source:** [Dave Says: Using Your Emergency Fund](https://cbn.com/article/finances/dave-says-using-your-emergency-fund) (Dave Ramsey, CBN, Mar 24, 2023).
- **Attribution:** Dave Ramsey directly.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 42 — Where to keep it: liquid, boring accounts — never investments

- **Principle:** The emergency fund lives in a checking or money market account with check-writing/debit access (current guidance: high-yield savings/money market); it is not invested.
- **Explanation:** Rachel Cruze's guide: keep it "liquid... get to it easily and quickly," in "a simple checking account or money market account that comes with check-writing privileges," and explicitly not in a place where market swings could hit it. The 2026 Baby Step 3 page says a high-yield savings account.
- **Reasoning:** The fund's value is certainty and immediacy, not yield; investing emergency money defeats its purpose.
- **Applies to:** Both the $1,000 starter fund and the 3–6 month fund.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None.
- **Source:** [A Quick Guide to Your Emergency Fund](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/saving/quick-guide-to-your-emergency-fund), section "Where should I keep my emergency savings?"; [Baby Step 3 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-3).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey personality (Cruze) / Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No (opportunity cost is acknowledged by critics but the liquidity logic is standard).

### Finding 43 — After using the fund: pause and rebuild immediately

- **Principle:** If the emergency fund is drained, rebuilding it becomes the immediate priority — pause other goals (like the snowball) and refill.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions' rebuild guide: go back into intense, short-term mode (budget, cut spending, sell things, extra income) until the fund is restored, then resume the interrupted Baby Step. The Baby Step 6 page repeats this for later steps: "refill your emergency fund if you ever have to use it."
- **Reasoning:** The fund is the plan's insurance; an empty fund exposes the whole plan.
- **Applies to:** After any emergency-fund drawdown.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Rebuild intensity matches the original Baby Step 1/3 intensity — temporary, not permanent austerity.
- **Source:** [How to Rebuild Your Emergency Fund](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/saving/what-to-do-after-using-emergency-fund) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021); [Baby Step 6 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-6).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 44 — Why only $1,000 during debt payoff: intentional discomfort

- **Principle:** The starter fund is deliberately small so it creates urgency to finish Baby Step 2 fast.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions: "Having only $1,000 sitting there should ignite your drive to pay off that debt even faster... the point is not to be in debt that long." The article also instructs people holding large savings while in debt to strip it down: someone with $50,000 debt and $23,000 savings should keep $1,000 and throw $22,000 at the debt.
- **Reasoning:** Comfort with a big cushion plus debt equals slow debt payoff; discomfort plus focus equals speed.
- **Applies to:** Baby Step 1–2 households, including those with existing savings.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Critics and some planners argue $1,000 is too thin a buffer for high-deductible health plans, volatile incomes, or families with children — see Disputed Claims.
- **Source:** [Is a $1,000 Emergency Fund Enough?](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/is-starter-emergency-fund-enough) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021), sections "How Much Should I Have in My Emergency Fund?" and "Why $1,000 Is Enough."
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** Partially. See Disputed Claims.

### Finding 45 — Emergency fund sizing is about expenses, stability, and volatility — with published calibration rules

- **Principle:** Size = essential monthly expenses × 3–6, where the multiple rises with household volatility.
- **Explanation:** Published calibration (Rachel Cruze's guide): two-income household or multi-year steady job → 3 months probably fine; one-income family, self-employed, straight commission → 6 months; chronic medical condition in the house → aim for 6. The 2026 step page: steady job → 3; varying paycheck → 6.
- **Reasoning:** Match the cushion to the probability and duration of an income shock.
- **Applies to:** Baby Step 3 target-setting.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** The "expenses" basis (not income) means the number is usually smaller than people assume.
- **Source:** [A Quick Guide to Your Emergency Fund](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/saving/quick-guide-to-your-emergency-fund), section "How big should my emergency fund be?"; [Baby Step 3 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-3).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey personality (Cruze) / Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

## SECTION E: Investing and Retirement

### Finding 46 — The 15% rule and the account funding order

- **Principle:** Invest 15% of gross household income for retirement, in this order: (1) up to the employer match in the workplace plan, (2) fully fund Roth IRAs (each spouse), (3) back to the workplace plan until 15% is reached.
- **Explanation:** "Dave's Investing Philosophy" page lays out the sequence; the 2026 Baby Step 4 page adds the math (annual gross × 15% ÷ 12) and repeats that the goal is "regular maintenance," not gazelle intensity: "the goal here isn't gazelle intensity—it's regular maintenance."
- **Reasoning:** Capture the match first (free money), then use Roth accounts for tax-free growth, then fill the remainder in the workplace plan.
- **Applies to:** Baby Step 4 onward.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** A Roth 401(k) with good fund options can absorb the entire 15%. The 15% figure deliberately excludes college savings and extra mortgage principal (those are Steps 5–6).
- **Source:** [Dave's Investing Philosophy](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/daves-investing-philosophy) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021), section "A Simple Investing Plan"; [Baby Step 4 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-4).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions (page presents Dave's philosophy).
- **Disputed:** No (the 15% level is within mainstream guidance; the timing rules are disputed separately).

### Finding 47 — Growth stock mutual funds, split equally across four types

- **Principle:** Invest in "good growth stock mutual funds" spread across four categories: growth, growth and income, aggressive growth, and international — 25% each.
- **Explanation:** "Dave divides his mutual fund investments equally between each of these four types of funds" (Dave's Investing Philosophy). The Baby Step 4 page (2026) repeats the four-way split as "the Ramsey recipe." Diversification is across fund types, not asset classes in the conventional sense — Dave's approach uses equities for essentially the whole portfolio.
- **Reasoning:** Spread across fund styles to reduce single-fund risk while keeping overall equity growth.
- **Applies to:** All Ramsey-plan retirement investing.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Debt-free real estate purchased with cash is the other approved investment (Baby Step 7). Bonds, CDs, annuities, single stocks, and REITs are excluded (Finding 52).
- **Source:** [Dave's Investing Philosophy](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/daves-investing-philosophy), section on mutual funds; [Baby Step 4 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-4).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions (presented as Dave's own allocation).
- **Disputed:** YES — the near-100% equity allocation and active-fund approach are disputed. See Disputed Claims.

### Finding 48 — How to pick funds: track record, manager tenure, costs, turnover — and front-end load funds

- **Principle:** Select funds using six criteria: objective (one of the four types), manager experience (5–10+ years), balanced sector diversification, long-term performance (10+ years, consistently outperforming category peers), cost, and turnover ratio (≤50%).
- **Explanation:** On cost, Ramsey Solutions states: "We recommend front-end load funds. With this type of fund, you pay fees and commissions up front when you make your investment" — while also advising that expense ratios above 1% are expensive. The performance criterion looks for funds that "consistently outperform most funds in its category" over 10+ years.
- **Reasoning:** Experienced managers with low turnover and long track records are expected to persist; paying costs up front keeps money "from being bogged down" later.
- **Applies to:** Fund selection inside 401(k)s and IRAs.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** The article concedes new managers can be acceptable if the fund has performed consistently. The front-load recommendation and the persistence assumption are both strongly disputed (Finding 49 and Disputed Claims).
- **Source:** [How to Choose the Right Mutual Funds](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/how-to-choose-the-right-mutual-funds) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Mar 12, 2021), all six sections.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** YES — see Disputed Claims (active vs. index, load funds, performance persistence).

### Finding 49 — The 12% return claim and Ramsey's own defense of it

- **Principle:** Dave Ramsey teaches investors can expect roughly a 12% average annual return from good growth stock mutual funds.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions' own explainer ("Return on Investment; the 12% Reality") traces the number to the S&P 500's average annual return since 1923 — 12.25% through 2016 — and notes nearer-term averages of 10.72% (1992–2016) and 11.66% (1987–2016). It addresses the 2000–2009 "lost decade" (−1% average) by pairing it with the 1990s (18% average) and argues expectations must be based on long-term history.
- **Reasoning:** The number is presented as the historical arithmetic average of the S&P 500, used to motivate early, consistent investing.
- **Applies to:** Retirement projections and motivation.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Ramsey's own article concedes "12% is not a magic number" and that the lost decade existed.
- **Source:** [Return on Investment; the 12% Reality](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/the-12-reality) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021), sections "Where Does the Idea of a 12% Return on Investment Come From?" and "But What About the 'Lost Decade'?"
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions (defending Dave's claim).
- **Disputed:** YES — one of the most-criticized claims in personal finance. See Disputed Claims for the full evidence.

### Finding 50 — The 12% dispute: arithmetic vs. geometric returns, fees, inflation, and persistence

- **Principle:** Independent analysts reject 12% as a planning assumption.
- **Explanation:** The documented criticisms: (a) 12% is the ARITHMETIC average; the compound (geometric) return is what investors actually receive — roughly 9.8% long-run for the S&P 500 per the NYU dataset Ramsey cites (per Yahoo Finance coverage of PGIM's David Blanchett, who called the 12% figure "absolutely nuts" for ignoring volatility and ~3% inflation); (b) McLean Asset Management found the market's compound return exceeded 12% in only 5 of 113 rolling 40-year periods (~4% of the time); (c) SPIVA scorecards show ~93% of domestic equity funds underperform their index over 15 years, undermining both the 12% and the active-fund approach; (d) fund performance barely persists, so past winners don't keep winning. Kitces' counterpoint: the 12% assumption is behaviorally effective as a motivational device (FOMO on compounding) even though technically indefensible.
- **Reasoning:** Planning on 12% risks undersaving and over-withdrawing in retirement.
- **Applies to:** Retirement projections.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Kitces notes the claim's motivational function for beginners.
- **Source:** [Yahoo Finance — Expert slams Suze Orman and Dave Ramsey's 12% returns claim](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/absolutely-nuts-expert-slams-suze-210000688.html) (Bethan Moorcraft, Feb 5, 2024); [TheStreet — Dave Ramsey's investing advice falls short, study finds](https://www.thestreet.com/crypto/markets/dave-ramseys-investing-advice-falls-short-study-finds) (Bibhu Pattnaik, Oct 2, 2026); [Kitces — Ramsey's 12% Stock Returns Assumption](https://www.kitces.com/blog/dave-ramsey-show-financial-peace-university-behavioral-finance-criticism-debt-snowball-bengen-12-percent-return-withdrawl/) (Jan 27, 2021).
- **Attribution:** Independent analysts.
- **Disputed:** This finding documents the dispute.

### Finding 51 — Buy and hold; never market-time; stay calm in downturns

- **Principle:** Invest consistently every month, hold for the long term, and do not sell in downturns.
- **Explanation:** Dave's Investing Philosophy: "Keep a long-term perspective"; ETFs are viewed warily partly because they tempt market timing, while "Dave prefers a buy-and-hold approach with a long-term view of investing." The 2026 Baby Step 4 page: "The stock market will rise and fall in the short term—but over time, it's had a proven track record of growth... Slow and steady wins every time."
- **Reasoning:** Time in the market and consistent contributions beat prediction attempts.
- **Applies to:** All Ramsey-plan investors.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None — this is the least controversial Ramsey investing principle.
- **Source:** [Dave's Investing Philosophy](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/daves-investing-philosophy), ETF and real-estate sections; [Baby Step 4 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-4).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 52 — Excluded investments: single stocks, bonds, CDs, annuities, gold, crypto, REITs

- **Principle:** Ramsey's approved list is short — mutual funds (four types) and paid-for real estate; nearly everything else is excluded.
- **Explanation:** Dave's Investing Philosophy excludes single stocks (one company failure wipes you out), bonds and CDs (poor long-term growth), variable annuities (fees, surrender charges, lost growth), and REITs (prefer direct, debt-free real estate). Documented on-air statement (2024, via TheStreet): "I have three investments, that's all I have. My business, paid-for real estate and mutual funds. I don't play single stocks. I don't screw around with gold. I don't mess with Bitcoin." The Total Money Makeover also advises: "I recommend you stay away from all variable and fixed index annuities. The returns are not worth the high fees" (quoted by Ark Wealth Management).
- **Reasoning:** Complexity, fees, and speculation don't serve the simple, long-term, equity-based plan.
- **Applies to:** All investing under the plan.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Money market funds are fine for emergency funds; HSA investing appears in Ramsey's insurance content as a tool.
- **Source:** [Dave's Investing Philosophy](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/daves-investing-philosophy); [TheStreet — Dave Ramsey's investing advice falls short](https://www.thestreet.com/crypto/markets/dave-ramseys-investing-advice-falls-short-study-finds) (documented 2024 on-air quote); [Ark Wealth Management — Dave Ramsey's Conflict of Interest](https://www.ark-wealth.com/blog/dave-ramseys-conflict-of-interest-many-smartvestor-pros-sell-fixed-index-annuities-he-rejects) (Nov 2025) quoting The Total Money Makeover.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions + Dave Ramsey directly (on-air quote).
- **Disputed:** The exclusion of bonds (100% equities at all ages) is disputed — see Disputed Claims.

### Finding 53 — The 8% withdrawal rate in retirement — Dave's most-attacked investing position

- **Principle:** Ramsey has repeatedly told listeners they can withdraw 8% annually in retirement (12% assumed return minus 4% assumed inflation), and has publicly attacked the 4% rule.
- **Explanation:** Documented on-air statement (November 2023, quoted by InvestmentNews): "If you're making 12[%] in good mutual funds, and the S&P has averaged 11.8[%], and if inflation for the last 80 years has averaged 4% … that leaves you 8[%]. If you want to be a little bit conservative, 7[%], but sure not 5[%] or 3[%]." He also dismissed 4%-rule researchers as "nerds" and "goobers" and said "There are a lot of studies that are stupid in this space" — after his own co-host George Kamel had published 4–5% guidance (Ramsey: "I'm just irate right now that we have joined the stupidity"). The Total Money Makeover states: "If you invest your nest egg at retirement at 12% and want to break even with 4% inflation, you will be living on 8% income" (quoted by Kitces, who also cites Ramsey's site formula: desired annual income ÷ 0.08 = required nest egg).
- **Reasoning:** Follows mechanically from the 12% return assumption.
- **Applies to:** Retirement income planning.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Ramsey's site has also suggested 7% as "a little conservative." Kitces notes the guidance is ambiguous between a constant-dollar rule (dangerous) and a percentage-of-portfolio rule (much safer).
- **Source:** [InvestmentNews — After Dave Ramsey unleashes on 4% rule, advisors unleash on him](https://www.investmentnews.com/retirement-planning/after-dave-ramsey-unleashes-on-4-rule-advisors-unleash-on-him/246454) (Emile Hallez, Nov 30, 2023); [Kitces — Ramsey's 8% Distribution Rule](https://www.kitces.com/blog/dave-ramsey-show-financial-peace-university-behavioral-finance-criticism-debt-snowball-bengen-12-percent-return-withdrawl/).
- **Attribution:** Dave Ramsey directly (on-air quotes documented in press).
- **Disputed:** YES — severely. See Disputed Claims.

### Finding 54 — Roth IRA: the favored account for tax-free growth

- **Principle:** The Roth IRA is Ramsey Solutions' "favorite option" for retirement saving after the workplace match.
- **Explanation:** The how-to-start guide: "When you hear the word Roth, we want you to think happy, because a Roth IRA allows your money to grow tax-free" — after-tax contributions, tax-free qualified withdrawals after 59½. The guide covers contribution limits, income phase-outs, and opening through a broker or investment pro.
- **Reasoning:** Paying tax now and never again on growth is the preferred structure, especially for people expecting higher taxes or brackets later.
- **Applies to:** Baby Step 4 investors.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Income limits apply; high earners use the workplace plan (or backdoor Roth, which Ramsey content also covers).
- **Source:** [How to Start a Roth IRA](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/how-to-start-a-roth-ira) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Mar 12, 2021), sections "Why Open a Roth IRA?" and "How to Start a Roth IRA."
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 55 — ETFs and index funds: not the preferred tool

- **Principle:** Ramsey Solutions prefers actively managed mutual funds over ETFs and index funds.
- **Explanation:** Dave's Investing Philosophy treats ETFs as acceptable baskets but warns they invite market-timing behavior; the article "Index Funds vs. Mutual Funds" (in the retirement hub) argues for actively managed funds, and Ramsey has said on air, "It's just not that hard to beat the S&P 500" (documented via TheStreet).
- **Reasoning:** Belief that good managers add value and that buy-and-hold discipline is easier with mutual funds.
- **Applies to:** Fund selection.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None — this is one of the clearest divergences from mainstream advice (SPIVA data: ~93% of active domestic funds underperform over 15 years).
- **Source:** [Dave's Investing Philosophy](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/daves-investing-philosophy); [TheStreet — Dave Ramsey's investing advice falls short, study finds](https://www.thestreet.com/crypto/markets/dave-ramseys-investing-advice-falls-short-study-finds).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions + Dave Ramsey (on-air quote).
- **Disputed:** YES. See Disputed Claims.

### Finding 56 — Work with an investment pro — "the heart of a teacher" — via SmartVestor

- **Principle:** Ramsey consistently directs followers to investment professionals (SmartVestor Pros), with the standard that the pro educates rather than sells.
- **Explanation:** Dave's Investing Philosophy: "Even though Dave has a thorough understanding of how retirement investing works, he still prefers to work with a financial advisor... their most valuable role is keeping you on track." In a Dave Says column (Madill Record), Dave told a veteran to "find a good investment professional near you—one with the heart of a teacher" for his TSP rollover. The SmartVestor program is a paid referral network.
- **Reasoning:** Behavior management and education are the advisor's core value; investors should "call the shots."
- **Applies to:** All Baby Step 4+ investors.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Fee-based vs. commission-based compensation is described as acceptable in either form if transparent. Critics document conflicts: SmartVestor is pay-to-play, and some referred advisors sell products (e.g., fixed index annuities) that Ramsey himself condemns — see Disputed Claims.
- **Source:** [Dave's Investing Philosophy](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/daves-investing-philosophy); [Dave Says column, Madill Record](https://www.madillrecord.net/node/10303); [Ark Wealth Management — SmartVestor conflict analysis](https://www.ark-wealth.com/blog/dave-ramseys-conflict-of-interest-many-smartvestor-pros-sell-fixed-index-annuities-he-rejects).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions + Dave Ramsey directly (column).
- **Disputed:** YES (conflict-of-interest criticisms). See Disputed Claims.

### Finding 57 — Delaying investing until debt-free: the deliberate trade against compound interest

- **Principle:** Investing waits until Baby Step 4 — even though this forfeits years of compounding and any employer match.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions' defense: "Millions of people try to multi-task... Not only does that slow their progress, it also leaves them unprepared when unexpected expenses arise," citing Forbes-reported data that workers without emergency funds are six times more likely to take 401(k) loans in emergencies. The company argues pausing investing speeds debt elimination and prevents the retirement-raiding cycle.
- **Reasoning:** Behavior and focus first; math second. (See Findings 9, 31.)
- **Applies to:** Baby Steps 2–3.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** The 2026 step pages and Investing Philosophy page still teach the delay. Critics (White Coat Investor, Kitces, most fee-only planners) accept the logic for people with genuine debt problems but reject it as a universal rule — especially the forfeited employer match, which is an immediate 50–100% return.
- **Source:** [Pay Off Debt Before You Save For Retirement](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/pay-off-debt-before-retirement) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021); [Should I Take My 401(k) Company Match?](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/401k-company-match); [White Coat Investor — Dave Ramsey Criticism](https://www.whitecoatinvestor.com/22-things-dave-ramsey-gets-wrong-and-right/).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** YES. See Disputed Claims.

## SECTION F: Major Financial Decisions (Housing, Cars, Credit, College, Insurance)

### Finding 58 — Mortgage rules: 15-year fixed rate, payment ≤ 25% of take-home pay, 10–20%+ down

- **Principle:** Buy a home only with a 15-year fixed-rate mortgage, with a total monthly payment (principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and HOA if any) no more than 25% of take-home pay, and a down payment of at least 10%, ideally 20%+ to avoid PMI.
- **Explanation:** The Ramsey Solutions affordability guide walks the math: monthly take-home × 25% = maximum payment, then a mortgage calculator example on a 15-year fixed (e.g., $5,000 take-home → $1,250 max payment → roughly a $211,000 home at 20% down). The Motley Fool/Nasdaq summary of Ramsey's home-buying readiness list: no other consumer debt, 3–6 month emergency fund, down payment saved, housing costs ≤ 25% of take-home, and no plans to move within about three years.
- **Reasoning:** The 15-year forces faster payoff and lower total interest; the 25% cap preserves budget margin for other goals; PMI is wasted money.
- **Applies to:** All home purchases under the Ramsey plan.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Paying cash for a house is presented as the ideal ("Cash is king, baby!"). See Finding 59 for rent-vs-buy timing.
- **Source:** [How Much House Can I Afford?](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/real-estate/how-much-house-can-i-afford) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021), section "Calculate the Price You Can Afford Based on Your Income"; [Should You Rent or Buy a Home? Here's What Dave Ramsey Thinks](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/should-you-rent-or-buy-a-home-heres-what-dave-ramsey-thinks) (The Motley Fool via Nasdaq, Jun 16, 2022, summarizing Ramsey Solutions' guidance).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** YES — the 25%-of-take-home-plus-15-year combination is criticized as unattainable in many current US housing markets. See Disputed Claims.

### Finding 59 — Why a 15-year mortgage and never longer (Dave Says, directly)

- **Principle:** 15-year fixed mortgages only; never 30-year, never ARM.
- **Explanation:** In a verified Dave Says column on 15 vs. 30-year mortgages, Dave wrote: "the normalization of the 30-year mortgage has helped create a constant state of financial bondage for the middle class... It costs just a few dollars more—like 20 percent more than you'd pay on a traditional 30-year mortgage. Plus, it gets you out of debt at least 15 years earlier," adding that 15-year borrowers have a higher probability of paying off early "because they can see light at the end of the tunnel." On ARMs (another verified column): "Adjustable rate mortgages are a bad, bad deal. If you currently have one I'd encourage you to refinance now!" — the ARM transfers interest-rate risk to the consumer.
- **Reasoning:** Debt duration is the enemy; shorter terms build equity faster and end the bondage sooner.
- **Applies to:** All mortgage decisions.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Refinancing a 30-year into a 15-year is endorsed; refinancing frequently is fine "as long as each time you do a refinance you're moving to a better rate" (per the Dave Says column on refinancing in the same CBN series).
- **Source:** [Dave Says: A 15 vs. 30 Year Mortgage](https://cbn.com/article/finances/dave-says-15-vs-30-year-mortgage) (Dave Ramsey, CBN, Jan 15, 2023); [Dave Says: Adjustable Rate Mortgages](https://cbn.com/article/finances/dave-says-adjustable-rate-mortgages) (CBN, Mar 24, 2023).
- **Attribution:** Dave Ramsey directly.
- **Disputed:** Partially — the 30-year vs. 15-year tradeoff (payment flexibility vs. total interest) is debated; see Disputed Claims.

### Finding 60 — Renting is not "throwing money away": rent while you get ready

- **Principle:** Renting is a legitimate, even smart, choice while you save a down payment, pay off debt, and build the emergency fund.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey's readiness criteria (Finding 58) mean many households should rent: "If you do not meet these criteria, he suggests putting your homeownership dreams on hold and renting while you get into a better financial position" (Motley Fool summary). The Living Without a Credit Score guide adds: "Rent for a while if you need to and save up even more to put into a down payment. And whatever you do, don't buy a house until you're ready" — defined as out of debt, 3–6 months saved, and a good down payment.
- **Reasoning:** A house bought too early becomes "a burden—not a blessing" that blocks wealth building.
- **Applies to:** Anyone not yet meeting the readiness checklist.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Buying is encouraged once the checklist is met and the buyer expects to stay several years.
- **Source:** [Living Without a Credit Score](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/living-without-credit-score) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Mar 4, 2021), "Buying a Home" section; [Motley Fool/Nasdaq — What Dave Ramsey says about renting vs. buying](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/should-you-rent-or-buy-a-home-heres-what-dave-ramsey-thinks) (Jun 16, 2022).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions (via Fool summary).
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 61 — "I almost never tell people to sell their homes" — the big-shovel principle (Dave Says, directly)

- **Principle:** Do not sell your home to pay off debt unless you truly cannot afford it or it is the only way to avoid bankruptcy; a large income is a "big shovel" that can dig out of a deep hole.
- **Explanation:** In a verified Dave Says column, an anesthesiologist ($260K income, $50K retirement, $50K consumer debt, $220K student loans, $280K mortgage) proposed draining retirement, selling the house, and renting. Dave's reply: "I almost never tell people to sell their homes. If you actually can't afford it, that's one thing... You're in a pretty deep hole, but your income... gives you a really big shovel... We're going to temporarily stop adding to your retirement fund, not cash it out, and we're going to start living on a written, monthly budget... beans and rice, and no vacations." Dave's plan: put $90,000/year toward the $270,000 of debt and be done in about three years.
- **Reasoning:** Drastic asset sales are a last resort when income can service the debt; intensity and lifestyle cuts preserve retirement assets and the home.
- **Applies to:** High-income households with large debt considering radical resets.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Sell if the house is genuinely unaffordable or bankruptcy is the alternative.
- **Source:** [Dave Says column, Madill Record](https://www.madillrecord.net/node/10303) (Dave Ramsey, Aug 10, 2023), anesthesiologist letter.
- **Attribution:** Dave Ramsey directly.
- **Disputed:** No (widely praised even by critics as sound triage).

### Finding 62 — Cars: pay cash; total vehicle value ≤ half your annual income; never lease

- **Principle:** Buy cars with cash; the combined value of all household vehicles should not exceed half of annual income; leasing is rejected outright.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions: "The car you can afford is the car you can pay for in cash. And as a general rule, the total value of all your vehicles combined shouldn't be more than half your annual income." Leasing is called "fleecing" — "the most expensive way to drive a car," essentially a glorified rental. The pay-cash guide teaches saving a monthly "payment to yourself" (e.g., $7,000 car = $583/month for 12 months) and buying used.
- **Reasoning:** Car payments consume the income needed for wealth building; cars depreciate, so debt on them is doubly destructive; the wealthy model (Finding 63) is modest used cars.
- **Applies to:** All vehicle purchases.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Dave Says (directly): a new car is acceptable only at a net worth of at least $1 million — before that, "drive good used cars... model your financial behavior after people who are in the position you want to be in someday." A "really nice, barely used car" is fine after Baby Step 3.
- **Source:** [How Much Car Can I Afford?](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/saving/how-much-car-can-i-afford) (Ramsey Solutions, May 26, 2023); [How to Pay Cash for a Car](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/saving/pay-cash-for-car) (Nov 29, 2021); [The Truth About Financing a Car](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/the-truth-about-financing-a-car) (updated Feb 25, 2021); [Dave Says: When to Buy a Better Car](https://cbn.com/article/finances/dave-says-when-buy-better-car) (CBN, Mar 24, 2023).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions + Dave Ramsey directly (Better Car column).
- **Disputed:** No (the half-income rule is a conservative guideline, broadly accepted as directionally sound).

### Finding 63 — What the wealthy actually drive: the millionaire-next-door modeling principle

- **Principle:** Most high earners drive ordinary, modest vehicles — model your spending on people who are where you want to be.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions cites Experian Automotive data (via Forbes): 61% of households earning $250,000+ drive Hondas, Toyotas, and Fords; only 39% choose luxury brands, while 8% of sub-$100K earners own luxury cars ("You make $50,000 a year and you own a $60,000 car? That's insane"). The article ties this to Dave's "average millionaire" teaching: most millionaires keep working, live modestly, and drive unremarkable cars.
- **Reasoning:** Status spending is anti-wealth; behavior modeling beats aspiration.
- **Applies to:** All purchase decisions, especially vehicles.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** The statistic is about brand choice among high earners, not a rule.
- **Source:** [What Do Wealthy People Really Drive?](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/saving/cars-wealthy-people-drive) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 64 — Buying a home without a credit score: manual underwriting

- **Principle:** You do not need a credit score to get a mortgage — use a lender that does manual underwriting.
- **Explanation:** The Living Without a Credit Score guide: look for "a process called 'manual underwriting,' sometimes called 'nontraditional credit' or 'no credit score' lending." The file is built on a large down payment, stable employment history, and outstanding rental/utility payment history. For rentals: first and last month's rent plus deposit and landlord referrals.
- **Reasoning:** If you never borrow, a borrowing score is unnecessary; underwriting can verify ability to pay directly.
- **Applies to:** Debt-free households seeking mortgages or apartments.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Critics note manual underwriting is harder to shop for, some lenders won't do it, and it can mean worse pricing — see Disputed Claims. Ramsey Solutions itself notes the guidance is for people with NO score, not a bad one.
- **Source:** [Living Without a Credit Score](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/living-without-credit-score), sections "Buying a Home" and "Renting an Apartment."
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** YES. See Disputed Claims.

### Finding 65 — College: choose the affordable school, cash-flow it, and treat student loans as disqualifying

- **Principle:** Pay for college with cash, scholarships, grants, and work — at a school you can afford — and refuse student loans.
- **Explanation:** The Baby Step 5 page (2026): "The number one mistake students make? Choosing a school they can't afford," noting people call The Ramsey Show "buried under six figures of student loan debt." Ramsey Solutions' college content (e.g., the Borrowed Future podcast series featuring Dave Ramsey, Mark Cuban, and Rachel Cruze) teaches: start at community college, transfer to in-state public universities, live at home, apply heavily for scholarships, and work part-time. For graduates, student loans are snowballed like other debt; Dave Says (directly) has told parents they may generously pay off a child's loan as a gift — ideally with the child's written commitment to never borrow again except a 15-year mortgage.
- **Reasoning:** Loans are marketed as normal but create decades of payments; the debt-free degree path exists for undergraduates.
- **Applies to:** Students and parents.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Professional school (med, law) is the acknowledged hard case (Finding 36).
- **Source:** [Baby Step 5 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-5); [Borrowed Future: How to Pay for College Without Student Loans](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/saving/how-to-pay-for-college-without-student-loan-debt) (Ramsey Solutions); [Dave Says: Paying a Child's Student Loan](https://cbn.com/article/finances/dave-says-paying-childs-student-loan) (CBN, Jan 15, 2023).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions + Dave Ramsey directly (Dave Says column).
- **Disputed:** Partially (professional-school feasibility). See Disputed Claims.

### Finding 66 — Life insurance: level term life, 10–12 times income, 15–20 years — and "self-insure" after

- **Principle:** Buy level term life insurance equal to 10–12 times annual income, with a 15–20 year term; never whole life or cash-value products.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions: "Dave says get coverage that's equal to 10-12 times your annual income. And make it a level term life policy that lasts for 15-20 years." The payout replaces income while dependents are young; by term's end, the household should be debt-free with investments making life insurance unnecessary ("self-insured"). Whole life is described as "a total rip-off" — more expensive premiums for a cash-value component Ramsey's system makes redundant.
- **Reasoning:** Insurance is for catastrophic risk transfer, not investment; term is cheap, and the Baby Steps create the net worth that replaces the need.
- **Applies to:** Anyone with dependents; stay-at-home parents also need coverage (income replacement of household labor).
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Coverage needs rise with young kids and single incomes.
- **Source:** [How Much Life Insurance Do I Need?](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/insurance/how-much-life-insurance-do-i-need) (Ramsey Solutions, updated Feb 25, 2021), sections "How Much Life Insurance Do I Need?" and "What Does Life Insurance Cover?"; [Term Life vs. Whole Life Insurance](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/insurance/term-life-vs-whole-life-insurance) (updated Feb 25, 2021).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions (with "Dave says" framing).
- **Disputed:** The anti-whole-life stance is shared by most fee-only advisors; the commissioned SmartVestor channel is the disputed part (Finding 56).

### Finding 67 — Insurance protects every Baby Step: six protections always in place, umbrella at $500K net worth, LTC at 60

- **Principle:** Insurance is not a Baby Step — it is the protection layer for all of them. Maintain health, auto, homeowners/renters, life, long-term disability, and identity theft protection at all times; add umbrella liability around $500K net worth (roughly Baby Step 4); buy long-term care insurance at age 60.
- **Explanation:** The April 2026 Ramsey Solutions guide warns against cutting insurance to accelerate debt payoff: "Many people starting the Baby Steps cut insurance to the bare minimum while paying off debt because that feels logical... But this thinking creates a dangerous gap" — one accident, illness, lawsuit, or identity theft can wipe out years of progress. Umbrella rationale: home/auto liability limits stop near $500,000, so net worth above that needs excess liability coverage. LTC at 60 (not sooner, not later) covers nursing home and assisted-living costs.
- **Reasoning:** The plan's assumptions (emergency fund, no new debt) fail if uninsurable risks are transferred away.
- **Applies to:** Every household on the plan, at every step.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None — the article's entire point is that there are no Baby Step exceptions to coverage.
- **Source:** [When Should You Buy Insurance on the Baby Steps?](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/insurance/insurance-and-the-baby-steps) (Ramsey Solutions, Apr 7, 2026), sections "Why Insurance Isn't Connected to a Baby Step," "The 6 Insurance Protections You Should Always Have in Place," "When to Add Umbrella Insurance," "When to Buy Long-Term Care Insurance."
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

## SECTION G: Communication Style and Coaching Approach

This section documents HOW Ramsey communicates — tone, structure, motivational devices, and how he handles mistakes and competing goals — based on verified columns, company-published teaching, and documented on-air statements.

### Finding 68 — Core framing: behavior over math; "normal is broke"

- **Principle:** Ramsey consistently frames money problems as behavior problems, not knowledge or math problems.
- **Explanation:** Signature formulations across Ramsey Solutions content: "Personal finance is 80% behavior and only 20% head knowledge" (debt snowball articles); "When it comes to money, being normal means being broke... you want to be weird" (car payments article); "Debt is normal. And normal is broke. Be weird!" (Baby Step 2 page). The White Coat Investor, a frequent critic, lists "Personal finance Is Mostly About Behavior" as thing #1 Ramsey gets RIGHT: "getting into debt is caused by bad behavior and so getting out of debt cannot be accomplished until the behavior changes."
- **Reasoning:** People don't fail financially because they lack spreadsheets; they fail because of habits, so the remedy is habit change reinforced by quick wins.
- **Applies to:** The entire teaching approach.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Where behavior is already good (disciplined high-income professionals), the pure-behavior framing fits less well — the root of most technical criticism of Ramsey.
- **Source:** [How to Get Out of Debt With the Debt Snowball Plan](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/get-out-of-debt-with-the-debt-snowball-plan); [The Truth About Car Payments](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/the-truth-about-car-payments); [Baby Step 2 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-2); [White Coat Investor](https://www.whitecoatinvestor.com/22-things-dave-ramsey-gets-wrong-and-right/).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions (formulations) / Dave Ramsey (originator).
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 69 — Motivational vocabulary: gazelle intensity, beans and rice, "live like no one else"

- **Principle:** Ramsey motivates through vivid, memorable phrases rather than technical arguments.
- **Explanation:** Documented examples from captured sources: "You are gazelle intense" (debt snowball example — outrunning debt like a gazelle outruns a cheetah); "I'm talking about beans and rice, and no vacations" (Dave Says, Madill Record — extreme temporary austerity); "That's living like no one else so later you can live and give like no one else" (Baby Step 6 page — the plan's thesis statement); "punch that car loan in the face" and "Sallie Mae better get used to living somewhere else, because you've kicked her out!" (snowball example); "chainsaw to those chains" holding you back (401(k) match article); "Murphy repellent" (Baby Step 3 page — an emergency fund keeps Murphy's Law away).
- **Reasoning:** Memorable, emotional language sustains motivation through months of sacrifice; humor lowers defensiveness.
- **Applies to:** All Ramsey communication.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** The same bluntness reads as dismissive to sophisticated audiences (see Finding 53's "nerds" and "goobers" quotes).
- **Source:** [How the Debt Snowball Method Works](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/how-the-debt-snowball-method-works); [Dave Says, Madill Record](https://www.madillrecord.net/node/10303); [Baby Step 3](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-3) and [Baby Step 6 pages](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-6); [Should I Take My 401(k) Company Match?](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/401k-company-match).
- **Attribution:** Dave Ramsey / Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 70 — How Ramsey responds to mistakes: no shame, back on the plan, refill the buffer

- **Principle:** Mistakes (a drained emergency fund, a busted budget) are treated as recoverable events, not moral failures — the response is always to restart the plan with intensity.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey Solutions' rebuild guide is explicitly shame-free: "There's no shame in that game. This is what the fund is for—emergencies. It did its job—and well—so get back on those stairs." The debt-snowball pause guidance tells people to resume "once you're back on track with money and life." Dave Says columns consistently validate the person ("You've done a great job of managing your money"), then correct the behavior.
- **Reasoning:** Shame causes avoidance; a no-drama restart protocol keeps people engaged.
- **Applies to:** Budget failures, emergency-fund draws, debt-payoff stalls.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Ramsey is notably harder on what he sees as self-deception (e.g., keeping credit cards "for emergencies," overspending labeled as emergencies) than on honest stumbles.
- **Source:** [How to Rebuild Your Emergency Fund](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/saving/what-to-do-after-using-emergency-fund); [When to Pause Your Debt Snowball](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/3-reasons-to-pause-debt-snowball); [Dave Says: Using Your Emergency Fund](https://cbn.com/article/finances/dave-says-using-your-emergency-fund).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions / Dave Ramsey.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 71 — How Ramsey prioritizes competing goals: income protection first, focus second, intensity third

- **Principle:** When goals compete, Ramsey's hierarchy is: (1) protect survival and the plan's foundation (Four Walls, insurance, starter emergency fund), (2) refuse to split focus across goals, (3) apply temporary extreme intensity to the single current goal.
- **Explanation:** Worked examples captured in this research: an anesthesiologist told to pause (not cash out) retirement and sell nothing, just cut lifestyle to $90K/year of debt payoff (Finding 61); a couple wanting to invest while in Baby Step 2 told to stop all investing (Finding 9); a painter told a predictable slow season is a budget line, not a reason to reorder the Baby Steps (Finding 18); parents told to start the family rather than wait for perfect finances, with a pause-and-stockpile protocol when the baby arrives (Finding 10, Dave Says "First the Baby or the Debt?").
- **Reasoning:** Splitting effort multiplies timelines and saps motivation; concentrated, temporary intensity finishes goals fast.
- **Applies to:** Every tradeoff question the show receives.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Insurance is never sacrificed (Finding 67); Steps 4–6 run in parallel by design.
- **Source:** Synthesis of sources cited in Findings 9, 10, 18, 61, 67.
- **Attribution:** Dave Ramsey / Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** The strict sequencing is disputed (Findings 31, 57).

### Finding 72 — The generosity and stewardship frame

- **Principle:** The system's stated endpoint is giving, framed in explicitly Christian stewardship language.
- **Explanation:** The Baby Step 7 page: "money is for three things: giving, investing and having fun... you can relax, have fun and let God use you to bless others." Budget guidance starts with giving 10% to church or charity before any other line (Finding 14). The Dave Says column on paying a child's student loan asks for a signed commitment never to borrow again except a 15-year mortgage — framing debt freedom as a family legacy ("changed your family tree").
- **Reasoning:** Wealth without purpose fails to motivate; generosity is presented as the most fun you can have with money.
- **Applies to:** The plan's philosophical foundation.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** The 10% giving line is faith-based guidance, not a universal prescription.
- **Source:** [Baby Step 7 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-7); [How to Budget With an Irregular Income](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-budget-an-irregular-income); [Dave Says: Paying a Child's Student Loan](https://cbn.com/article/finances/dave-says-paying-childs-student-loan).
- **Attribution:** Dave Ramsey / Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 73 — Working the plan as a household: budget meetings and spouse unity

- **Principle:** Married couples budget together and make money decisions jointly; the plan is taught as a couples activity.
- **Explanation:** Ramsey's budgeting content repeatedly instructs married couples to go over the budget together ("make sure you go over the results with your spouse. You both need to be on the same page" — house affordability guide) and describes the budget meeting as a monthly ritual. The Dave Says corpus consistently addresses both spouses.
- **Reasoning:** Money conflict is a leading marriage stressor; joint planning prevents sabotage and builds accountability.
- **Applies to:** All married households.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None stated.
- **Source:** [How Much House Can I Afford?](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/real-estate/how-much-house-can-i-afford), section 1; [How to Make a Zero-Based Budget](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/how-to-make-a-zero-based-budget).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No.

## SECTION H: Decision-Making Synthesis — How Ramsey Chooses Between Competing Options

These findings distill the selection rules an AI mentor needs to answer "which do I do first?" questions the Ramsey way.

### Finding 74 — Smaller debt vs. higher-interest debt → smaller debt wins

- **Principle:** When a small-balance debt and a high-interest debt compete, pay the small balance first.
- **Explanation:** The snowball's entire design (Finding 27–28). Example from Ramsey Solutions' snowball article: a $500 medical bill is attacked before a 20%-interest card with a larger balance, because the medical bill dies in one month and fuels momentum.
- **Reasoning:** Completion probability beats theoretical interest savings; "there's power in behavior modification on a short-term basis that supersedes the power of mathematics" (Dave Says, Finding 9).
- **Applies to:** Always, under the Ramsey system.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None published. (Independent note: a disciplined household that will complete either plan should run the numbers on the avalanche — Finding 29.)
- **Source:** Findings 27–29 sources.
- **Attribution:** Dave Ramsey / Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** The math-vs-behavior tradeoff is the system's central disputed claim.

### Finding 75 — Emergency fund vs. debt payoff → tiny fund first, then attack debt; savings vs. debt → strip savings to $1,000

- **Principle:** $1,000 buffer precedes debt attack; beyond that, extra savings while in debt is stripped and thrown at the debt.
- **Explanation:** The Is-a-$1,000-Enough article: a person with $50,000 debt and $23,000 savings keeps $1,000 and pays $22,000 to debt immediately. The starter fund exists only to prevent new borrowing during payoff.
- **Reasoning:** Cash sitting idle while debt accrues interest is a negative carry; discomfort accelerates the payoff.
- **Applies to:** Baby Steps 1–2.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Extremely volatile incomes effectively need more cash (Finding 16's peak-and-valley fund is the published accommodation).
- **Source:** [Is a $1,000 Emergency Fund Enough?](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/is-starter-emergency-fund-enough).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** Partially — most advisors support a small fund first but differ on the amount and on stripping existing savings.

### Finding 76 — Investing vs. paying off debt → debt first, always, until non-mortgage debt is gone

- **Principle:** Even when expected investment returns or an employer match exceed the debt's interest rate, debt payoff comes first.
- **Explanation:** Documented in Findings 9, 31, 57. The company explicitly acknowledges forfeiting the match ("that queasy feeling is what's going to keep you working hard") and the compounding delay, and defends it on behavioral grounds (401(k) loan statistics for people without emergency funds).
- **Reasoning:** Focus and behavior change; eliminating risk of raiding retirement mid-plan.
- **Applies to:** Baby Steps 2–3.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** None published. This is the single most-criticized sequencing rule (see Disputed Claims).
- **Source:** Findings 9, 31, 57 sources.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** YES.

### Finding 77 — Sell assets vs. keep paying → sell if the payment outruns the rules

- **Principle:** Sell financed depreciating assets that can't be cleared within 24 months or that exceed ~50% of net worth; keep the house unless unaffordable or bankruptcy-threatening.
- **Explanation:** The two car rules (Finding 32) versus the near-never-sell-the-house rule (Finding 61). Depreciating collateral with oversized payments is jettisoned; the home is protected because it appreciates, provides shelter, and selling costs are high.
- **Reasoning:** Match the decision to what the asset does: depreciating luxuries get liquidated to free income; shelter is preserved and paid off through Baby Step 6.
- **Applies to:** Cars, boats, and similar financed assets during Baby Step 2; homes at all times.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Items within a few months of payoff are kept.
- **Source:** Findings 32, 61 sources.
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions / Dave Ramsey.
- **Disputed:** No.

### Finding 78 — Retirement vs. college → retirement first

- **Principle:** If funds are limited, fund retirement investing to 15% before college savings.
- **Explanation:** "Your kids may not even go to college, but you're definitely going to want to retire, and no one else is going to pay for that!" (Baby Step 5 page). Kids have options (scholarships, work, affordable schools); parents cannot borrow for retirement.
- **Reasoning:** Irreplaceable compounding time and no retirement loans.
- **Applies to:** Parents balancing Steps 4 and 5.
- **Exceptions/limitations:** Both steps begin only after Step 3; they then run concurrently.
- **Source:** [Baby Step 5 page](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-5); [Dave's Investing Philosophy](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/daves-investing-philosophy).
- **Attribution:** Ramsey Solutions.
- **Disputed:** No (mainstream advice generally agrees).

## SECTION I: Disputed Claims — Where Ramsey Differs From Conventional Advice

Each entry states Ramsey's position, the mainstream/competing position, the evidence, and the strength of the dispute.

### I-1. The 12% expected return (FINDINGS 49–50) — STRONGLY DISPUTED

- **Ramsey's position:** 12% average annual returns are a reasonable long-run expectation for good growth stock mutual funds, based on the S&P 500's average since the 1920s (his own article: 12.25% average annual return 1923–2016).
- **Competing position:** The 12% figure is an arithmetic mean; investors receive the geometric (compound) return, roughly 9.8% long-run for the S&P 500. PGIM's David Blanchett called the assumption "absolutely nuts" for ignoring volatility and inflation. McLean Asset Management found compound returns above 12% in only ~4% of rolling 40-year periods. SPIVA data show ~93% of active domestic funds underperform their index over 15 years, so a "good fund" reliably matching even the index is the exception, not the rule.
- **Evidence quality:** The critique is quantitative and multiply sourced (NYU data, SPIVA, Morningstar). Kitces' defense is behavioral, not mathematical — he concedes the number is technically indefensible but argues it motivates saving.
- **Mentor guidance:** Use realistic compound returns (~7–10% nominal for equities) for projections; if emulating Ramsey's motivational framing, do not present 12% as a planning input.

### I-2. The 8% retirement withdrawal rate (FINDING 53) — STRONGLY DISPUTED

- **Ramsey's position:** Withdraw 8% annually in retirement (12% return − 4% inflation), or 7% "to be a little conservative"; he has publicly mocked the 4% rule and its researchers ("nerds," "goobers," "There are a lot of studies that are stupid in this space").
- **Competing position:** Bengen's original 4% guideline (1994) and subsequent research (including Morningstar's State of Retirement Income, which put the "safe" starting withdrawal at 3.3–4.0% for a 90% probability of 30-year sustainability, 3.9% in the 2026 edition; Bengen himself raised his ceiling only to ~4.7% in 2025 after adding asset classes). A Bengen-style 8% inflation-adjusted withdrawal has historically carried a high probability of portfolio depletion. Kitces adds that Ramsey's rule is ambiguous between a constant-dollar rule (dangerous) and a percentage-of-portfolio rule (much safer, essentially cannot fully deplete).
- **Evidence quality:** The 4%–5% consensus is among the most-researched findings in retirement planning; the dispute is not close on the math. The Chattanooga Times Free Press's December 2023 analysis called the 8% advice "potentially hazardous" and about double responsible starting points.
- **Mentor guidance:** Never project retirement sustainability at 8%; use 3.5–5% depending on horizon and allocation.

### I-3. Skipping the employer match during Baby Step 2 (FINDINGS 31, 57, 76) — STRONGLY DISPUTED

- **Ramsey's position:** Stop all investing, including matched 401(k) contributions, until non-mortgage debt is gone.
- **Competing position:** An employer match is an immediate 50–100% return — better than any debt's interest rate. Most fee-only advisors recommend capturing it even during debt payoff. The White Coat Investor lists this among Ramsey's errors, and Kitces' checklist-style analysis notes the Baby Steps' sequencing is behaviorally powerful but technically suboptimal in exactly this way.
- **Evidence quality:** The match's return is arithmetic, not a projection. Ramsey's defense is behavioral (focus, and the 401(k)-loan risk of underfunded households).
- **Mentor guidance:** If strictly emulating Ramsey, state his position and its reasoning; a responsible mentor should also disclose the match tradeoff.

### I-4. Near-100% equity allocation at all ages (FINDINGS 47, 52) — STRONGLY DISPUTED

- **Ramsey's position:** Growth stock mutual funds (four types, 25% each) for essentially the entire portfolio; no bonds; 100% stocks regardless of age was his documented November 2023 on-air position.
- **Competing position:** Age-based or target-date allocations include bonds because equity volatility near retirement creates sequence-of-returns risk — the mechanism behind the 4% rule. Morningstar's research shows higher equity weightings do not raise the sustainable withdrawal rate under income-stability spending systems because volatility offsets expected growth. Christopher Hopkins (Chattanooga Times Free Press): "Betting the ranch entirely on risky assets increases the odds of losing the ranch."
- **Evidence quality:** Strong and consistent across retirement research.
- **Mentor guidance:** Present Ramsey's allocation as his position; do not recommend it as universally safe, especially near or in retirement.

### I-5. Active mutual funds, front-end loads, and performance persistence (FINDINGS 48, 55) — STRONGLY DISPUTED

- **Ramsey's position:** Pick actively managed growth funds with 10+ year track records; front-end load funds are recommended; "It's just not that hard to beat the S&P 500."
- **Competing position:** SPIVA scorecards (S&P Global): ~93% of domestic equity funds underperform their index over 15 years; S&P persistence data show past outperformance barely persists; front-end loads are a sales commission that reduces returns from day one. Low-cost index funds are the mainstream default.
- **Evidence quality:** Among the most robust datasets in finance.
- **Mentor guidance:** If emulating Ramsey, present his criteria; a responsible mentor notes the index-fund evidence.

### I-6. The debt snowball vs. avalanche (FINDINGS 27–29, 74) — MODERATELY DISPUTED (with partial support)

- **Ramsey's position:** Smallest-balance-first regardless of rate.
- **Competing position:** Avalanche (highest-rate-first) minimizes total interest; the snowball costs more in interest.
- **Evidence:** The snowball's behavioral advantage is REAL and documented — Kellogg's Gal & McShane found closing accounts (small first) predicted successful debt elimination — so this is a genuine tradeoff, not an error: completion probability vs. interest cost. Ramsey's own materials concede the math. The dispute is over which matters more for a given household.
- **Mentor guidance:** For low-discipline, overwhelmed households, snowball; for disciplined households with large rate spreads (e.g., 24% card vs. 3% student loan), at least quantify the avalanche's savings.

### I-7. Living without a credit score (FINDINGS 38, 64) — MODERATELY DISPUTED

- **Ramsey's position:** The credit score is an "I love debt score"; live without one; use manual underwriting for mortgages.
- **Competing position:** Credit scores affect more than borrowing: insurance pricing in many states, some employment screening, apartment applications, and utility deposits. Manual underwriting is available from fewer lenders, is harder to shop, and rates/pricing can be less favorable. Critics (widely documented in financial media, e.g., Moneywise and CBS coverage) call the advice impractical for most households.
- **Evidence quality:** Practical-friction arguments are well documented; the philosophical argument (no borrowing → no score needed) is internally consistent.
- **Mentor guidance:** Present Ramsey's stance accurately; flag the real-world frictions.

### I-8. The 25%-of-take-home, 15-year mortgage rule (FINDINGS 58–59) — MODERATELY DISPUTED

- **Ramsey's position:** 15-year fixed, total payment ≤ 25% of take-home pay, 10–20%+ down.
- **Competing position:** In many 2024–2026 US markets (median home price ~$408,800 per NAR data cited by U.S. News), the rule prices out median-income buyers in large metros; U.S. News asked "Is That Even Possible?" Redditor critiques (covered by Yahoo Finance) called it "nearly impossible" for non-wealthy buyers. The 30-year vs. 15-year tradeoff (flexibility, invest-the-difference) is also debated, though Ramsey's total-cost logic has supporters.
- **Evidence quality:** The affordability critique is empirical and current; the direction (housing should be a smaller share of income) is broadly endorsed.
- **Mentor guidance:** Keep the rule as Ramsey's benchmark; acknowledge market-context exceptions (longer timelines, higher-cost metros, renting longer).

### I-9. Student loans and professional school (FINDINGS 36, 65) — MODERATELY DISPUTED

- **Ramsey's position:** No student loans, ever, for undergrad; for professional school he points to scholarships, MD/PhD programs, service commitments, and need-based aid rather than advising against professional school.
- **Competing position:** Kitces: "almost no one is going to pay for a $200,000 education working part-time" — the strategies are "strained" for medical and law school. The White Coat Investor (a physician site) lists "You can't pay for medical school without debt" among Ramsey's errors, though it credits him for fixing his earlier PSLF (Public Service Loan Forgiveness) advice — for physicians pursuing nonprofit careers, income-driven repayment + PSLF can outperform aggressive payoff, a nuance Ramsey's blanket anti-loan stance omits.
- **Evidence quality:** Documented tradeoffs; both sides have cases.
- **Mentor guidance:** Present Ramsey's stance; note PSLF/IDR exceptions for professional-school borrowers, which even Ramsey's one-time co-hosted ecosystem has acknowledged.

### I-10. Debt consolidation is always bad (FINDING 33) — WEAKLY DISPUTED

- **Ramsey's position:** Consolidation moves debt, doesn't remove it; rates aren't guaranteed; behavior is the problem.
- **Competing position:** For a borrower who has already fixed their spending, consolidating high-rate balances into a lower-rate fixed loan mathematically reduces interest and can accelerate payoff. Ramsey's objection is completion risk (re-borrowing on cleared cards), which is real but not universal.
- **Evidence quality:** Both positions are defensible; this is a client-fit question.
- **Mentor guidance:** Frame as Ramsey does (behavior first), but a balance-transfer/consolidation with a closed spending loop is not irrational.

### I-11. SmartVestor and endorsed-provider conflicts of interest (FINDING 56) — MODERATELY DISPUTED

- **Ramsey's position:** Use vetted investment pros (SmartVestor), insurance agents (Endorsed Local Providers), and preferred refinancing partners.
- **Competing position:** The programs are pay-to-play referral networks; advisors pay for leads, which is a conflict Ramsey does not always disclose on air. Ark Wealth Management documented that some SmartVestor pros sell fixed index annuities that Ramsey's own book condemns. The White Coat Investor: "A commissioned salesman is not a real financial advisor."
- **Evidence quality:** The referral-fee structure is documented; the annuity inconsistency is documented with examples.
- **Mentor guidance:** Do not imply Ramsey's recommended pros are fiduciaries; disclose the referral-for-fee structure if the mentor emulates his recommendations.

### I-12. $1,000 starter emergency fund adequacy (FINDING 44) — WEAKLY TO MODERATELY DISPUTED

- **Ramsey's position:** $1,000 is enough during debt payoff because the debt should be gone in 18–24 months and the small fund creates urgency.
- **Competing position:** With high health-insurance deductibles, car repair inflation, and family obligations, $1,000 can be breached by a single event, restarting the cycle; some planners recommend one month of expenses as the starter buffer.
- **Evidence quality:** Reasonable-practice debate rather than hard data.
- **Mentor guidance:** Present Ramsey's number and its rationale; a one-month-expenses buffer is a defensible adaptation for volatile households.
